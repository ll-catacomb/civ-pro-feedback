## Question 3

 The DPDFLA has some positive components, but overall it isn’t a good idea.
DPDFLA institutes notice pleading, lowering the pleading standard and increasing

accessibility. The use of the internet for communications makes the system easier to

access and potentially less time-consuming for litigants. But major benefits of this

technology aren’t realized because live hearings and oral testimony are excluded.

 Conducting all proceedings by writing may disadvantage parties with less formal

education. Additionally, parties are allowed to use lawyers to craft their writings. This

would be a large disadvantage for those who cannot afford counsel. While no oral

testimony removes the potential concern of unrepresented litigants being mistreated by

judges (Lassiter), it also prevents judges from leveling the playing field for those

unrepresented (by using inquisitorial judging). A system conducted entirely by writing

might be opaque and confusing, and the lack of a hearing provides little opportunity for

an unrepresented litigant to get answers and understand the process. Moreover, the power

imbalance resulting from a lack of counsel would be more hidden from the court, and

thus potentially less likely to be reformed.

 The lack of formality with regards to evidence could simplify procedures. But it

risks a system that is difficult to understand for first-timers, where rules are ad-hoc and

confusing. Therefore, simplified, easy to follow procedures, like the ones in Turner where

specific questions and determinations were required, are preferable.

344122 344122

 Preventing party-joinder provides much needed simplification. However, since

joining claims and counterclaims is allowed, we may have compulsory counterclaims. If

they are and claim preclusion would then run if they aren’t asserted, the harm to

unrepresented litigants could be grave.

 Removing discovery removes complication, but it likely privileges those with their

own resources to conduct investigations. Sometimes discovery is imperative to
determining the merits of a claim, even in slip and fall cases like Dolgencorp, so it’s

absence is concerning. Also, not allowing appeals means no review of potentially

erroneous decisions. The three-judge panel alleviates this concern somewhat, but

allowing appeals would still be valuable, especially since the scheme allows pre-

adjudication seizure of property by a single ALJ and injunctions.

 Scheme also doesn’t preserve jury trial if ALJ’s can’t sit juries. Juries are valuable

for infusing community standards into the process.

 Because it adds procedural problems to the system and fails to alleviate the

fundamental disparity between unrepresented and represented litigants, this scheme isn’t

a good idea.
