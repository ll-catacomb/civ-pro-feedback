## Question 7

 (a)The new specialized court with exclusive state-level jurisdiction is valiantly

attempting to respond to access to justice concerns for pro se litigants. In a post-Turner

world, it is clear that access to justice initiatives with require creative solutions. The

benefits of this new specialized court are not outweighed by the potential harms; broader

nation-wide adoption of such courts and PDP improvements, see B, would be needed as

well as special preclusion rules. Prompt says “appear in front of the judge.” I will proceed

under assumption of no jury.

 The proposal should be commended following ideological footsteps of the New

York and San Antonio, which have created courts for pro se litigants, see Barton/Bibas.

Not all civil suits require an attorney: for example, divorce suits with parties in agreement

do not need attorneys who fight [this court would sweep in lower-value divorce suits].

 Without a jury and attorneys to slow trial and increases costs, the economic and

time efficiency returns of specialized court could benefit both private interests and

government interests, Matthews. For plaintiffs with low-value lawsuits, the decreased

costs could enable individuals to bring civil claims that they otherwise could not bring

without a class-action. While an increase in lawsuits is not always a good thing, a

plaintiff’s ability to bring low-value suits should be seen as an access to justice win

897730 897730

because plaintiffs have an opportunity to be heard and have legal issues resolved. For

government interests, an initial investment would be required to build out and provide

training for the specialized court system, but the long-run costs savings could more than

off-set the short-run government expense.

 While such a system has great intentions, Harlan’s concerns in Hanna about pre-

litigation behavior and Erie’s twin aim concerns about forum-shopping and inequitable

administration of justice should be considered. If our state were the first to adopt this

specialized state courts, we could incentivize in-state businesses that have claims under

$200k to move out of state in order to access federal courts [collections agencies, for

example], where pro se defendants would be left without aid.

 If this court does not provide juries for legal claims, we should be concerned losing

the community values, such as reasonableness for negligence, and witness credibility

assessments that juries can provide. We should be wary of sweeping all civil claims into

this system. Low value medical malpractice claims may be too complicated for the

system. Special preclusion rules would be needed: how to divide lawsuits with same T/O

and claims both under and over $200,000. We would need exceptions for class actions.

For the limited subset of individuals who do have attorneys, we should be wary of

limiting access to justice by taking away their legal help [even if removing their attorney

would assist a pro se opponent].

 (b) Despite these, specialized state courts could be desirable with modifications that

comport with PDP and improve access to justice. A court system without attorneys will
