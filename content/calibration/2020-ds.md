## Question 6

 Example 1: In claim preclusion doctrine, a prior judgment is regarded as final as
long as a trial judge had reached that judgment. This reveals deep faith in trial judge
judgments, because we allow them to have preclusive effects even if those judgments
might be appealed. This belief is justified given the trade-offs the court system must
make between limited judicial resources and an interest in accuracy. If the system placed
less faith in the accuracy of trial judge judgments, we would have a lot more appeals,
preclusion would run less often resulting in more lawsuits - this volume of cases would
be unmanageable, cause huge delays, and decrease access to justice.

 Example 2: Under FRCP50, a trial judge may issue a judgment notwithstanding

the verdict, setting aside the jury verdict. Giving the trial judge this power validates the
court system’s perception of the judge as omniscient: his determination of what a rational
jury would do trumps what an actual jury did. In the context of JNOV, this belief is not
justified. The recognition of the judge as the arbiter of rationality open ups the potential
for abuses of power in the context of JNOVs and undermines juries’ role as reflecting
community standards and representing the common person’s perspective.

## Question 7

 The court system could collaborate with institutional sectors (healthcare, education,
social services) to set up public legal education and information centers in cities, towns,
and virtually. These centers would offer free information on court procedures and advice
on legal and non-legal solutions to problems. The drawbacks follow: while this multi-
disciplinary approach may redistribute some of the burdens currently imposed on a
tightly-stretched court system to other sectors, it would still be costly to set up centers
and to train and support individuals providing free advice. It is difficult enough to train
lawyers to provide good advice, that it is unlikely institutional partners can get up to
speed on providing sound legal advice. Problems of inequity would persist: even if per se
litigants can enter the court system better informed, it is unlikely they can tackle a legal
issue anywhere as robustly as a well-represented litigant or corporation.
