7. Courts have structured the law of collateral estoppel (CE) with the incentives of parties

in mind. The requirement that the issue be actually litigated is one example. Issue

preclusion does not require that parties litigate all the possible issues arising from a t/o

because a structural incentive to litigate every possible issue would create a huge problem

for the court system. Therefore, courts have shaped common law to prevent this costly

incentive from arising. The necessary to the decision requirement takes into account

parties' incentives to litigate issues well, reasoning that the party might not litigate an

issue fully if it is not necessary to the decision. The FCL of NMCE from Blonder-Tongue

and Parklane relies heavily on incentives. Parklane generally does not allow ONMCE
because it incentivizes a second plaintiff to sit on the sideline and see if the first plaintiff

could win. Courts wish to disincentive this behavior because it does not further judicial

efficiency or fairness. DNMCE, though, is generally allowed, because it incentivizes a

plaintiff to sue both defendants in one lawsuit, furthering judicial efficiency.

 FRCP 23 in the context of Amchem is an example of a rule that courts will view

strictly despite the incentive of the parties and attorneys. Amchem requires a class action
solely for purposes of settlement to go through the entire Rule 23 class certification

process. Here, the incentive of the defendant was to obtain a settlement. Though some

asbestos-exposed plaintiffs may have objected to the settlement, overall, an

administrative scheme similar to the one proposed would vastly decrease litigation costs

and increase the pool for plaintiff recovery. Attorneys would also gain more money.

Justice Ginsburg insisted on adherence to Rule 23, noting the legislature was best fit to

investigate these themes. (Amchem). Here, concerns about separation of power and the

proper role of courts seem to cause the Court to construe FRCP 23 strictly.

 Courts should take incentives into account, but must do so carefully and in a limited

context. Taking incentives into account can help further important judicial goals. In CE, it

promotes judicial efficiency, a major interest of the court system. Taking incentives into

account also plays an important role in promoting sectors of the economy and leads to

economic advantages. For example, claim preclusion law incentivizes a market in bonds.

Taking incentives into account also furthered the ideas of federalism and equal protection

in an Erie context. Justice Brandeis sought to eliminate incentives to forum shop and alter

business strategies by eliminating the Swift regime.

 Taking incentives into account, though, poses dangers. As Ginsburg noted, it might

cause courts to infringe upon the role of the legislature, creating separation of powers
issues. Taking incentives into account might also hurt the overall perception of the legal

system as an impartial arbiter of conflicts and encroach on due process concerns. We

believe strongly that all plaintiffs should have their day in court. Courts must take parties'

incentives into account through their Constitutional limit on hearing cases and

controversies, not resolving general issues. As a result, if courts always looked towards

incentives, courts might shape doctrine based on incentives in one case that would make
it more difficult for another party with different, but equally valid, incentives to bring a

case.

 In some cases, courts might have reliable sources of law. Former decisions and

subsequent parties' actions, such as the conduct in Black&White Taxicab after Swift, can

illuminate incentives reliably. Courts also might have impartial economic data, such as

the effects of rulings on bond markets. However, perhaps more often, courts will have

limited evidence to determine what incentives are, especially in cases of first impression.

Here, courts will likely have to rely on what the parties convey their incentives are

through their action or through evidence gathered during discovery. While sworn

affidavits and depositions might be reliable, I worry that relying on the parties' own

evidence leads to the potential for manipulation. This cautions against taking incentives

into account in order to render an impartial decision.

 Incentives can be an important factor to take into account. However, they should be

taken into account carefully and only when a court can rely on evidence not produced by

the actual parties to determine the incentives.
