Notice pleading, as exemplified by Form 11, only requires alleging enough facts and conclusions of law to put the defendant on notice as to what the claim is and the grounds upon which it rests. The proposed rule definitely requires a lot more than what is needed to put a defendant on notice that they are being sued for non-payment of their credit card debt. Code pleading requires pleading facts sufficient to allow the court to look at the complaint and see that if the plaintiff proves that these facts are true, the defendant is liable. In that way, the proposed rule is similar to code pleading. However, this rule goes far beyond code pleading in that not only must the plaintiff allege enough facts to show that the defendant is liable, she must attach evidence of these facts in her complaint! In this way, the new rule also goes beyond the “plausibility” standard of Bell Atlantic and Iqbal. The rule is somewhat in line with the ideas of those cases, in that it is also aimed at protecting defendants. However, whereas in those cases the rationale for a stricter pleading standard was to protect the defendants from wasting time and money on likely wasted discovery, the rationale here is to protect the due process rights of the defendants. In this way, the rule, while arguably procedural, seems to create a new substantive right for the debtor.
Different standards for specific actions make sense. As a debt collector, if you cannot show documentation that you legally own this person’s debt, it is highly unlikely you can prove your case, since the evidence in these cases is mostly documentary. The proposed rule, however, makes no sense for a tort negligence case, since the evidence in these cases is mostly witness testimony. Furthermore, you can use the pleadings to create a protection that levels the playing field in cases where it is likely to be highly uneven in terms of legal knowledge and access/adequacy of representation, as is done here, and could be extended to foreclosure and other similar cases which are also mostly based on documentary evidence. However, this rule should not be extended to all contracts claims, especially those between commercial parties, since many of these are based on oral bargaining and other communications, for which a lot of the evidence is testimony and unofficial documents (and the playing field between such parties is level, regardless). The main disadvantage of different standards is that they create an issue as to which standard should apply to particular not-so-clear-cut cases, which would likely cause significant increase in litigation time and costs on 12(b)(6) motions.
Alternatives to this rule which would protect the due process interests could be to require state-appointed counsel if the defendant does not have access to counsel (provided collector seeks to recover in excess of some amount, in order to conserve state resources). Another alternative is set up a workshop (with state resources) to teach debtors how to represent themselves, and require that information about the workshop be attached to all complaints served to defendants in such cases. However, both options are likely to be expensive for the State, and options like the second one are not likely to be very helpful, given the complexity of not only substantive law, but court procedure. The proposed rule is definitely the cheapest, and likely a very effective, way to protect the due process interests of debtors.
Where physical liberty is not at stake, there is a presumption that the state is not required to provide counsel. In such cases, the Matthews factors—private interest, risk of error, public interest—should be applied on a case-by-case basis to determine whether counsel needs to be appointed. Lassiter. In such cases, the private interest is usually significant—an adverse judgment could result in wage garnishment (and consequent loss of job), bankruptcy, default on other debts, foreclosure, etc. The risk of error without counsel is extremely high. Credit contracts are extremely complex and full of provisions someone without training is highly unlikely to be able to understand. Furthermore, (without rule 126) the defendant will likely have no idea what forms they need to request during discovery. The public interest in not providing counsel is also extremely high, however, since there are tons of these cases filed every day, and providing counsel would be very expensive. However, given that most cases are voluntarily dismissed if the collector finds out the debtor has counsel, if the state declares that it will provide counsel in all such cases, there will probably be a large decrease in the number of claims filed. However, even providing counsel in all the remaining cases would likely be too expensive, so the best compromise would be to provide counsel on a case by case basis where personal interest is significantly high (weigh the amount sought vs. the debtor’s “financial factors” (income, other debt, number of dependents, etc.), and provide counsel if it exceeds a certain amount.) This way, the state does not have to commit to the expense of providing counsel in every case, but knowledge that the state will provide counsel in some cases will still likely deter collectors to a significant degree from filing suits they don’t have good grounds to litigate, thereby protecting the due process interests of all debtors.
It would probably be hard to make the argument that the proposed rule is required by DPC. The best argument would be by looking to the Vitek factors, which state that an opportunity to present and cross-examine evidence is required by DPC. Given that most 50% of debtors don’t show up to court, and the 45% who represent themselves probably have no idea what forms they need to request in discovery, which probably means they never really get a chance to cross-examine the collector’s evidence, nor do they know what evidence of their own they need to bring to counter this evidence, it can be said that the presentation and cross-examination of evidence never occurs in the vast majority of these cases. However, DPC only requires a fair opportunity, it does not require that this actually happen, and the opportunity to show up to court and to request the paperwork in discovery is certainly present. Therefore, this rule is not required by DPC, even in the absence of counsel.
Since this rule would make it harder and more expensive (have to gather all the paperwork) for debt collectors to obtain judgments, the demand for debt will probably drop. This would make it less profitable for creditors to sell off their bad debt, so they would likely become more discriminate in selecting their clients and set lower credit limits. Overall, credit would become harder to obtain.

Commonality is as well, since the factual background of each claim is largely the same, and any differences will not affect the common remedy that they are all seeking.
