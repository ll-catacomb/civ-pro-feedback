 Q6

 For establishing complete diversity, we treat classes as a newly created entity.

The new entity adopts only the citizenship of the named plaintiff. (Supreme Tribe).

Where we normally require all plaintiffs to be diverse from all defendants, in class

actions, there can be thousands of nondiverse class members.

 However, when unnamed class members are AIC insufficient, we treat classes as

a joinder on steroids which has to comply with restrictive 1367 diversity rules. When

named plaintiff is completely diverse (from single defendant) and AIC sufficient,

supplemental jurisdiction 1367(b) can be exercised over unnamed class members who

individually do not meet AIC. (Exxon)

 But while AIC deficient claims don’t contaminate the original jurisdiction

lawsuit, under 1367(b)they do contaminate the lawsuit when they aren’t diverse. In this

respect, we force AIC insufficient claims to comply with joinder rules, whereas we don’t

apply those rules when only looking at complete diversity/aic sufficient claims.

 For specific PJ, it’s unclear whether we treat classes as a new legal entity or

joinder-case. Under Shutts, courts can exercise IPJ over unnamed members as long as

there’s procedural protections like notice, opportunity to participate, adequate

2XXXXX 2XXXXX

representation. But Bristol Myers Squibb left unanswered whether courts can exercise

specific PJ over unnamed plaintiffs when only some of the class have minimal contacts

(International Shoe).

 If considered a new legal entity, wherever class members suffered injury would

count towards minimal contacts, meaning it would be easier to establish specific PJ. In

that sense the class operates like a trust. If treated as a joinder, it would be much harder to

establish minimum contacts and specific jurisdiction because in large suits, plaintiffs are

in almost every state.

 I believe it’s better to treat classes as newly created legal entities because it

increases efficiency and makes litigation more likely. Class actions are intended as a

remedy for plaintiffs and so we worry less about applying arbitrary laws when they class

members can only gain from litigation. If we treat it as joinder, defendants (usually

companies who do bad things) can easily overcome litigation, creating an A2J problem.

Especially for damages classes under 23(b), if plaintiffs opt into litigation they’re

signaling consent to personal jurisdiction. A middle ground would be to treat opt-in

classes as legal entities but mandatory class actions as joinder-cases. Lastly, FRCP 23’s

commonality, numerosity, typicality and representativeness requirements provide legal

safeguards for defendants worried about unfairness.
