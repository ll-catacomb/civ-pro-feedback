## Question 9

 Change 1: Eliminate the Atlas Roofing’s public rights exception. Per Atlas Roofing,
non-Article III adjudication doesn’t require a jury trial. Argument is that juries
requirement would be expensive & slow bureaucracy even further. However, the blanket
denial of right to jury in these instances is egregious-- Eg, agency adjudications within
federal prisons, over matters as severe as solitary confinement and parole, should warrant
jury trials, not tribunals by COs. I think the public would be abhorred at the oft biased
decisions of CO adjudicators. Juries are unpaid anyway, but I do worry about expense &
the time/effort required to assemble a jury, but I think there are some novel ways to
reduce this. For instance, there could be a more efficient process of having a queue of
available jurors ready ahead of time to make convening a jury easier. Likely, Congress, or

even POTUS executive action, could nullify Atlas (at least on federal level). Or,
SCOTUS could overturn Atlas (saying 7th Amendment absolutely applies to
administrative tribunals, given framers intent & novelty of the administrative state.)

 Change 2: Overturning the Title v. US interpretation of FCRP 60(a). Title holds that
Rule 60 relief from a judgement or order cannot be granted merely because SCOTUS has
since interpreted the law differently than when rendering court ruled. For example, if an
African-American was found against for violating a segregation law pre-Brown, that

728801 728801

individual couldn’t seek relief once SCOTUS found that segregation is unconstitutional.
Title rationale is that finality is valuable because we can’t have endless litigation & we
need to stop somewhere. I agree Courts are overburdened, but suggesting that a party

cannot be granted relief from a judgement/order that is now understood to violate that
party’s rights seems patently unfair. And why would we want the system to keep
expending effort to enforce a ruling/order decided for reasons now understood to be
incorrect, even if judge was following precedent at the time. Change could come from a
number of places-- most likely either from Congressional statute granting right for relief
on this ground, or a SCOTUS case overturning Title.
