## Question 1
 a) Under the outcome-determinative test (York), the case will be dismissed if SoL is
2 years and will survive otherwise, so the case is dismissed under state law. Under the

Harlan concurrence in Hanna, we find that SoL doesn’t alter primary (non-lawyer)
conduct since SoL only comes into play after you’ve decided to file a lawsuit, so the case
survives under federal rule. Both Scalia and Ginsburg would find that the federal rule is
valid because it “really does regulate procedure” (Sibbach) and thus does not conflict
with the REA. It concerns what period of time a plaintiff must initiate a lawsuit in.
Regarding applicability, MA sets a shorter SoL than the federal rule, so Scalia would
likely find there to be a conflict, so the case survives under the federal rule. Ginsburg
might interpret the MA SoL as specifically pertaining to the death or injury of pets, for
which there is no federal equivalent, so there is no conflict and the state rule applies (and
case dismissed).

 b) Stevens in Shady Grave would actually apply the validity test beyond a nominal
citation to Sibbach by finding that FRCP 87 enlarges the scope of a substantive right

defined by state law. In this case, SoL is substantive because it determines how difficult it
will be for a plaintiff to seek remedy by setting a time limit (and Stevens cites Guaranty
Trust as an example of a state procedural rule creating a substantive right). Stevens would
then apply the same applicability test as Ginsburg to dismiss the case under state law.

 c) I think the court should apply state law and dismiss. Doing so upholds
constitutional values albeit at the expense of uniform federal procedure. However, I
believe preserving federalism and state-specific policy-making is more important than

creating a national market for lawyers. Furthermore, even the RDA, which Scalia relies
heavily upon in formulating his Erie test, presumes that state law applies unless Congress
“require[s]” otherwise. In this case, since MA specifically passed a law concerning cases

involving pets, it’s not clear whether the general federal rule actually requires us to

## Question 2
 On balance, I think class actions are not useful to promoting access to justice. On
one hand, because Turner/Lassiter together basically rule out the right to counsel in civil
cases, a would-be plaintiff might still be able to seek remedy as an unnamed member of a
class if she doesn’t want to appear pro se in litigation. Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur and
Exxon both set class actions up to allow this, by eliminating the diversity of citizenship
and AIC requirements for unnamed plaintiffs.

 However, class actions are ultimately still an efficiency-motivated litigation
mechanism, allowing (and sometimes requiring) claims to be consolidated. An unnamed
plaintiff has very little control over the litigation process and, in 23(b)(1)/(2) lawsuits,
cannot opt out if they want to. Furthermore, even in 23(b)(3) lawsuits, a plaintiff may not
have adequate notice of an ongoing class action to make any meaningful decision

pertaining to the lawsuit. And though the plaintiff also has a choice to opt out of (b)(3)
lawsuits and pursue the litigation on her own without being precluded, someone who
needs class actions as an access to justice mechanism probably doesn’t have other viable
options. Therefore, by failing to provide plaintiffs with a meaningful choice in how they
pursue litigation before precluding them from making future claims, class actions can’t
reasonably be construed as an alternative avenue to justice.

## Question 6

 The transfer motion is not immediately appealable. The court has not rendered a
final judgment under 1291. No injunction, so no 1292(a)(1). No further explanation
provided, so no double discretion (1292(b)) or FRCP 54(b). No irreparable harm or

order is final and collateral to the merits, it is not effectively unreviewable later, as the
right to be sued in a particular forum is not “essentiall destroyed” by allowing the trial
finish first (Lauro Lines wrt. forum selection clauses).

## Question 8
 Rule 54(b) was applied correctly. We have multiple claims: different legal theories
(negligence and conversion) against different parties (Sylvania and Zeppo) (Liberty
Mutual). The negligence claim is severable from conversion. Besides, Marcal’s judgment
will be reviewed with “substantial deference” (Curtiss-Wright). Finally, Marcal expressly
directed entry of final judgment and found there was “no just reason for delay”.

 Sylvania cannot invoke claim preclusion against Groucho. We have the same
parties (Groucho and Sylvania) and a judgment that is final and “on the merits”
(Sylvania’s liability in Groucho’s negligence claim). However, the theft of “Duck in
Soup” is not the same T/O as the theft of another artist’s work (“To War!”). Contra
Gonzalez, allowing preclusion here would inhibit the market for digital art.

 Sylvania might try using ONMCE on Groucho’s negligence/inability to recover.
First, we have mutuality, since these are the same parties in the same procedural posture.
Second, the issue was actually litigated in the MSJ proceedings. It’s unclear if SJ grant is
sufficiently firm, since they can be revised at any time (but hardly ever are). Finally, in
diversity cases, we apply the preclusion law of the state where the rendering federal court
sat (Semtek). However, since this case was transferred from TX to DE, it’s unclear which
state that is. Since TX was likely “unhappy”, and the plaintiff shouldn’t get their COL
when IPJ/venue are improper, DE preclusion rules should arguably apply.
