1 (b).
There are a number of normative questions lurking inside of this one, of which I will select two: a more procedural question, and a sweeping symbolic question. First, to understand what case a state or federal court should, it helps to look at the purposes behind federal jurisdiction. The constitution provides jurisdiction in federal arising under claims and diversity claims; extrapolating the values implicit in these two zones of jurisdiction, one sees, first, that federal courts are experts at federal issues, designed to create uniform law on federal questions, and second, that federal courts are perceived as more impartial—less susceptible to the everyday prejudices of states, to xenophobia, to the lesser parts of the American mind. Federal courts are typically down the street from state courts: this perception of neutrality is symbolic, mostly, and yet it is powerful, affirmed by cases like Cooper v. Aaron, a synecdoche for the best of the federal court.
The first purpose of the court, expertise and uniformity, suggests this case should be in federal court: it is only because the Mottley supersedes the sweeping jurisdiction suggested in Osborne that there would be no FQJ. And why not? In the Mottley case itself, the constitutional question ended up at the Supreme Court after multiple appeals: it is a testament the judicial inefficiency of the rule. Resolving such federal questions creates uniformity and clarity for the state courts; yes, one can argue that the Supreme Court will eventually hear important questions (and deliver uniform results), but a defensive issue, however appropriate, cannot get through Mottley, and in any case cert is difficult to achieve. Forcing uniformity when questions come up in defenses to rest entirely on a capricious, limited Supreme Court is to ask a funnel to hold back a river when the Constitution provided us a dam. I acknowledge that there is a powerful counter-argument: we do not want endless cases in federal court, nor do we want a murky standard to replace the heretofore clarity of federal jurisdiction. Nor do we want to upset the balance of the federal and state governments, also important and implicit in the constitution. Still, the court has developed standards (Matthews v. Eldridge) when such standards promoted fairness; here, altering the Mottley rule would not only lead to efficiency, uniformity, and clarity: it would level the federal playing field between plaintiffs and defendants, lest only plaintiffs be able to take advantage of limited, but powerful federal tool.
What about the symbolic purpose of the courts, however, as impartial agents? I think that purpose cuts against letting this kind of case into federal court. State courts are, by implication, more apt to reflect local values, and thus, to be experts on local questions (one of the arguments behind a Colorado Stay). Torts are profoundly local: they are infractions of the social contract, not of a national social contract, but of that which binds together a community. Mal-practice tort claims are no less local—the locality rule (a torts rule not discussed in Civ Pro) holds doctors subject to the customs of their locality, not to the nation. Barring diversity jurisdiction, I see a powerful symbolic reason to leave torts in the hands of the states. A tort is an adjudication of community values; we do not want impartiality, which is why we so often turn to a jury to resolve complex questions, and we do not uniformity. The tort laboratory of democracy leads to many different rules, perhaps, but a powerful process at the heart of federalism. Perhaps there is an argument here that an agency or panel of experts, rather than a jury, undermines this symbolic element to a tort claim—but I don’t see how that makes a federal judge, especially in equity, a better adjudicator.

First, there’s a PJ question here. There is no GJ over Mungo’s in NH, but there may be SJ. A statute can’t go beyond the constitution, but it can provide notice to a party that a state will exercise jurisdiction (see Mcgee). Of the 302 requirements, Mungo meets 302b (it advertises in NH newspapers), doesn’t meet 302a, and may meet 302(c) (we can ask our client). Mungo’s chose to advertise in NH despite have read the statute (see Pomfrey’s critique). Is that enough? Pince was not a resident of NH when the initial relationship began (most likely—see domiciliary discussion); she chose to move to NH on her own, which undercuts personal availment (see Hanson). Other factors a court will consider: our interest in not litigating in NH is low (same law, fairly close); so is Pince’s. Both of us have resources to travel (she now has a benefactor). But Pince saw the NH advertisement, which is why she came to the hospital. I think a court will likely find SPJ.
Was the lien constitutional? Our case isn’t quite Doehr, so let’s apply Matthews factors. The private interest here for Mungo’s is strong: the amount is unbelievably high, Mungo’s creditors are hungry, and, as a hospital, were we to lose access to our facilities, lives could be lost. Do the 503(e) factors mitigate. Dicta in Doehr suggests a bond is not enough to eliminate the need for a hearing, and putting wealth in an account would freeze up potentially liquid assets (see Cromwell).
Second, risk of error? Like in Doehr, we get no adversarial hearing (which helps our argument); unlike in Doehr, this is a code, not conclusory, plea (see 502); the plaintiff must add to it affidavits by experts; and the “deciding officer (DO)” makes sure they’re experts. That said, the DO seems (it’s ambiguous) to be appointed by Agency Board members and aren’t judges (they may or may not be more qualified than clerks; see Sniadich), and no one reviews the actual amount the plaintiff is asking for (the experts merely affirm negligence). The agency didn’t deliberate very thoroughly: it took them only one night/morning to determine that the property should be seized, and we didn’t get to make any kind of argument: Sniadich suggests strongly that taking property without giving us a hearing may be unconstitutional. There is a reasonably strong case that this system, in contrast to a hearing in which we get to at least file an answer or respond to affidavits, is rife with error.
Third factor: Pince has an interest in getting her award, but there is no exigent circumstance here; a huge hospital is unlikely to sell all of its assets or be unable to pay in the event that it loses the suit. And the government’s interest? In Doehr, the government seemed to have an interest in not attaching property unrelated to a suit, but that was a quasi-in-rem, not in personam, suit, and here, the real estate is more related to the suit than in Doehr. The government may also have an interest in not having a huge hospital potentially shuttered.
Next, we can argue that we have a 7th amendment right to a trial by jury. Whether there were malpractice suits when the Const. was signed I’m not sure of; there were certainly tort claims. Here, Pince is requesting compensatory and even punitive damages, again pointing us towards a jury (see Chauffeur). This is not a public right, it is a private party suing for damages; the agency didn’t create this right, it took control over it. That said, do we really want a jury? Judges award more often for plaintiffs, but juries award more.
Other Matthews attacks? Could we argue that the entire agency proceedings violate due process considerations when weighed against a jury or even judge adjudicated trial? Private interests: same as above. First, the lack of appealability is not a problem per se (Vitek), but in this proceeding, questions of law are appealed to the Agency Board, and thus never even looked at by a judge, which may create a potential for error. The rest of the proceedings allow both parties significant time to craft pre-trial motions, but, unlike in actual equity cases, the DO need not articulate her rulings on law and fact (see Roberts v. Ross). The state’s interests may outweigh: the state seeks judicial economy, efficiency, speed, fairness to indigent parties (see Turner) and, broadly, preventing massive mal-practice costs.

Is this claim or issue preclusion? Claim: we have a final judgment (a dismissal on the merits), and appeals don’t stop preclusion. We have the same parties. Same T and O: here we have the same central question (negligence per se), the same amount of damages, and the same facts. Could this be issue preclusion? Probably not: the issue of negligence wasn’t actually litigated.
Would the agency have to give claim preclusive effect? 806 suggests the agency must act as if it is a state court on the question of preclusion. Here, then, the dismissal on the merits should kill the suit. If the federal court had FQJ, the agency will certainly choose not to preclude in light of its authorizing act.
