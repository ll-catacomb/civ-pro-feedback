## Question 7

Federal courts straddle a fine line between creating clear rules that provide adequate

notice while not encouraging parties to “work” rules in a way that discourages judicial

economy and fairness. SCOTUS took a hard line approach in Owen Equipment v Kroger.

In this case, the anchoring claim was based on divjur, but the defendant impleaded a TPD

who was believed to be diverse. The Plaintiff unknowingly sued the TPD, which was

discovered to destroy complete diversity so the claim was dismissed. SCOTUS’ narrow

reading of the rule is an interesting example of basing decisions off incentives because

the actual Plaintiff was guileless in this situation but SCOTUS was concerned about

future Plaintiffs gaming the system. They narrowly interpreted the rule in order to deter

Plaintiffs from impleading parties later on so that they could get into federal court. In a
way, by not examining an individual parties’ incentive, SCOTUS preserved the right to

make a rule that would disincentivize future parties from gaming the system. SCOTUS

demonstrated that certain ideals like complete diversity and maintaining federal/state

balance should be free from strategic litigation.

Owen is one of the few cases to survive the passage of 1367 because the court was
forward thinking about how impleading could change party manipulations. As DivJur

continues to fade in comparison to FQJ as the primary purpose for Federal Courts,

balancing parties’ incentives is an important way of keeping too many cases from getting

swept in by DivJur.

However, courts haven’t always been so visionary or responsive to party machinations. In

Black/White v Brown/Yellow Taxicab, the court observed blatant maneuvering by parties

in order to take advantage of favorable laws. Contracts were allowable under federal law,

but not under state law. Thus, the Plaintiff reincorporated in another location to allow

itself the opportunity to litigate in federal court using favorable laws. Despite Holmes’

enraged dissent, the court chose to ignore this undisputable evidence of forum shopping

because it would mean confronting vertical uniformity issues within federal courts

(which they would be forced to do just a decade later). Another instance of a court

refusing to take a clear stance on how parties’ incentives could be shaped by law is

McIntyre v Nicastro. By finding no IPJ existed when the country as a whole was targeted,

companies might structure their distribution in a way that evades IPJ (i.e. liability) in any

state.

A general trend amongst these cases is that courts are initially hesitant to include parties’

incentives in their rationales for making decisions in an effort to not disturb the status

quo. However, a string of enraging cases generally follow where parties’ misconduct is

unavoidable and forces the court to re-think their original position. If we revisit

metaphysical Erie rationale, judges make law, they don’t “discover” law. Therefore,

parties’ incentives are a real concern when considering how one decision shapes future
litigation either between states or between federal and state courts. Furthermore, courts

want to incentivize judicial economy and fairness, and if they do not consider incentives

it could be to their detriment. For instance, if ONMCE is always allowed (Parklane), we

risk parties “waiting and seeing” before starting their own trial, which is not only unfair

to a defendant who must now litigate multiple times but is a waste of judicial time and

money. Furthermore, by reducing the ability to “game” the system, we could be creating

courts that allow individuals to better navigate the judicial system without a lawyer

thereby increasing access to justice. However, the dangers of shaping laws purely off

party incentives also negatively affect efficiency and fairness. If suits are too concerned

with creating the right incentives, we might lose some of the focus on the actual merits of

the case, which is unfair to the parties in question (e.g. Owen). For example, if parties are

forced to intervene becasue we are too worried about "wait/see" then we might have

over-litigation and monster-sized lawsuits. It also creates difficulties in creating rules

applicable to everyone because incentives are wide-ranging and is an inherently uphill

battle because companies that litigate an exorbitant amount (i.e. insurance companies)

will always find ways to forum shop and manipulate laws.

This becomes difficult considering information courts have to determine incentives.
Generally, courts will only have the facts in front of them to determine parties’ incentives

and these facts could tailored to individual circumstances, so don't provide much

foresight. Furthermore, studies on parties’ behavior might not always be reliable

depending on if they are transsubstantive, based on specific type of suits, or do not pull

on a diverse set of variables (differences in parties’ economic resources, geographic

locations, type of court). Courts could look at firms' stats to examine where/how firms
choose to litigate, but this again could be biased to certian types of suits.
