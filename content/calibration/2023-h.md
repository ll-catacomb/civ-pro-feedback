## Question 9

 Public rights exception, right to jury trial (Atlas Roofing). This exception exists
largely for administrative efficiency reasons; the government is party to lots of cases
when acting in its sovereign capacity and the number of cases to which it is party is only
increasing with the addition of new administrative agencies. Bench trials are more
efficient because they take up less of the parties’ and the court’s time than picking a jury,
seating a jury, instructing the jury, adjudicating issues outside the presence of the jury,
etc. However, these benefits likely do not outweigh the costs of stripping the non-
government party of the right to a jury trial. The jury serves as an equalizer, particularly
in domains where the government likely has an existing relationship with the court by
virtue of the fact that it is a repeat player. This may result in small but cumulative

benefits over time aggregating in favor of the government to the detriment of non-repeat
player non-government parties. For transition costs, Article III courts could implement
the switch to more jury trials quite easily, e.g., by allowing them if a non-government
party asks. However, adjudications in front of ALJs might pose more complicated
legislative and regulatory structural barriers to allow jury trials in cases otherwise entitled
to the public rights exception (but see Jarkesy v. SEC, currently pending SCOTUS case,
which might resolve this).

 Lansford-Coaldale interpretation of FRCP 52. Judges can read a party’s findings
of fact verbatim right after the close of evidence. This is largely seen as a win for

efficiency and for the court to be able to protect their record (e.g., by reading into the
record an arguably well-thought out statement of facts). However, one change that might
improve accessibility would be to require the judge to provide at least a short statement of
why they are adopting one party’s statement of facts (over the other’s). This could
improve accessibility by putting into the record the judge’s reasoning why they adopted
these facts as true, which could serve as data for future parties (assuming they had the
resources to look this up) as to what evidence and style of writing/statement of facts the
particular judge finds most compelling. However, this could raise the related accessibility
challenge of only making this benefit available to repeat players or well-off parties. That
said, the low cost of implementing it (judges adding such a brief statement to trial record)
weighs slightly in favor of adopting it, in case there is even one less-advantaged party
who can glean helpful strategy for appearing before the particular judge assigned to their
case.
