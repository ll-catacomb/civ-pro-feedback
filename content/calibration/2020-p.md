## Question 4

 XH and Faith could have appealed (within 28 days of entry of judgment--
deadline that cannot be extended under FRCP 6(b)(2)). Appeals only allowed for final
orders (§1292). Rule 52 says judges have to issue findings of fact and conclusions of law
separately on the record. The appellate court will use clearly erroneous standard. We have
rule 52 to (1) Improve quality of adjudication; (2) facilitate good review; (3) make
precedent clearer for preclusion. The third reason is relevant because issue preclusion
requires knowing what issues were necessary to the decision during fact-finding process.
Judges can adopt the findings from one of the parties but must issue fact and law
separately (e.g., Lansford Coaldale). Outcome unlikely to change due to clearly
erroneous standard for these findings.

## Question 6

 1. Pre-Judgment Seizure
 When evaluating a pre-judgment seizure for PDP violations (Matthews), a
process is deemed to be less likely to have violated PDP if a judge issued the writ of
garnishment rather than a clerk (Fuentes). The resulting risk of erroneous determination
is apparently very low because judges assume other judges do not make mistakes.
 The PDP doctrine about writs of garnishment is fair insofar as judges are
believed to be neutral arbiters, so they can weigh relevant factors about a lawsuit and the
property in question before issuing such a seizure. If judges are elected, they could decide
the writ on political, not judicial grounds. Appointed and elected judges alike are likely to
lack sufficient information about a pre-judgment attachment property before the lawsuit
begins to safely make an errorless assessment.

 2. Motions for Summary Judgment
 Trial judges engage in a burden-shifting framework when evaluating a motion for
summary judgment that asks them to view the facts in the light most favorable to one
party or another, sequentially in theory but using contemporaneously provided
information.
 Even though summary judgment may be efficient, historically, Rule 56 is said to
have been used only to weed out cases with quick answers or those that did not carry real

166190 166190

weight until the 1980s. We also discussed how the burden-shifting framework is
theoretical and judges are not likely to be following Brennan’s controlled instructions
from Celotex to guide them to what is assumed to be the right decision (but see Scott v.
Harris), and the final, on-the-merits nature of summary judgment decisions also has
lasting implications for litigants regarding claim preclusion.

## Question 7

 Allowing representatives beyond lawyers in court would expand access to
justice: for example, social workers (like J. Powell’s argument in Vitek). We discussed
that Washington state rolled out but revoked a non-lawyer representation option,
suggesting there might be impracticalities with implementation. A one-for-one swap
would not fix a supply-demand problem, but if lawyers were still allowed, there would
probably need to be criteria to decide whether a representative were a lawyer, social
worker, etc., in an attempt to “even the playing field” rather than have an adversarially
trained lawyer against an expert in another form of conflict resolution. Finally, with
criteria inevitably come difficult gray areas and lines to be drawn, that may well restrict
access even further if one type of representative is “better” or “worse” in quality than
another, on average.
