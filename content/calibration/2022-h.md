## Question 2

 Class actions promote access to justice by allowing persons with small claims to
have a cost-justified avenue for bringing suit by pooling their claims with others. There’s
potential for substantial damages in class actions (and substantial attorney’s fees) as the
defendents are often multinational corporations. Economies of scale allow classes to more
easily find/afford a lawyer on a contingency-based fee, which mitigates the need for state-
provided counsel. The Turner court worried about lawyers making proceedings more
complex and undercutting judicial efficiency, but class actions, by their nature, promote
efficiency in adjudicating many claims simultaneously.

 Class actions are not uniformly positive on access to justice. A class member can
get stuck with a settlement they view as inadequate which they’ll be forced to accept if

the court adjudges it “fair” under Rule 23(e). Individual suits could produce more
fulsome relief to some litigants, and yet some class action categories don’t allow class
members to opt out (FRCP23(b)(1)(B)).

## Question 6

 Groucho is unlikely to succeed in immediately appealing.

 Transfers aren’t final orders under §1291 (no judgment entered), so Groucho needs
to find an applicable interlocutory appeal.

 Cohen exception is most appealing. Cohen requires decision be collateral to the
merits (met here), final in that it won’t be revisited by court (technically D.DE could
revisit transfer, but unlikely), and issue must be effectively unreviewable later (here,
Groucho’s argument fails). Lauro Lines tells us SCOTUS viewed denial of a transfer
motion pursuant to a forum-selection clause as effectively reviewable later, so Cohen
didn’t apply. It logically follows that granting a transfer is reviewable later. Collateral

order doctrine isn’t concerned with saving costs of unnecessary litigation.

 Groucho’s case doesn’t fit other interlocutory appeals. There’s no written statement
seeking additional guidance (§1292(b)), no final judgment on any claim (FRCP 54(b)),
no injunctive relief (§1291(a)(1)), and no exigent circumstances (writ of mandamus).

 If an appeal immediately lies, Fifth Circuit would apply de novo review, as transfer
is question of law.

## Question 7

 Is contributory/comparative negligence procedural or substantive for HCOL
purposes? It seems substantive because establishing party’s right to recovery. However,
FRCP 8(c) lists contributory negligence as an affirmative defense, suggesting procedural.
Either way, comparative negligence controls.

 D.DE applies HCOL rules of state in which it sits (Klaxon). If affirmative defense
available is procedural, will use law of the forum state, Delaware, which uses
comparative negligence. If substantive, applying DE’s 1st Restatement principles
(Klaxon), we use law of state where “blood lies,” Texas, which uses comparative
negligence.

 Comparative negligence is an affirmative defense, so burden of persuasion lies with
Sylvania at trial. Judge Marcal will apply “burden shifting” analysis to MFSJ (Celotex, J.
Brennan dissenting). Sylvania meets its burden of production by producing evidence
from its engineer and Chico that Groucho had a simple password, showed it to Zeppo,
and didn’t use 2FA. Any rational factfinder would see this as failing to exercise due care
and contributing to potential theft. No genuine issue of material fact emerges, entitling
Sylvania to JAMOL. Burden of production then shifts to Groucho. Groucho’s assertions

802386 802386

that he’s forgotten his password, whether he wrote password down, and whether he used
2FA doesn’t create a genuine issue of material fact, because he doesn’t dispute Sylvania’s
assertions. Groucho fails to meet his burden of production. MFSJ granted to establish

Groucho as causal factor in theft.

 However, because DE/TX use comparative negligence, Groucho shouldn’t be
barred from recovering from Sylvania. Rather, his $100,000 claim against Sylvania will
be reduced by his comparative fault, a question of fact decided at trial. If Groucho
establishes Sylvania was also negligent, he can partially recover.

## Question 8

 Judge Marcal’s use of FRCP 54(b) was correct.

 Marcal complies with 54(b)’s requirements to expressly determine there’s “no just
reason for delay” and expressly direct judgment be entered. The only potential issue with
Marcal’s 54(b) determination is whether the Sylvania claim is intertwined with the Zeppo
claim. Because the MFSJ dealt only with Groucho’s comparative fault, and Zeppo cannot
assert that defense to a conversion action, the issues are arguably distinct. There are
factual similarities between the claims, but they’re likely not inseparable as to reverse a
Rule 54(b) determination (Sears).

 In evaluating issue preclusive consequences of Sylvania’s MFSJ, we note that the
rendering court was a federal court sitting in diversity. There are arguably two “rendering
courts,” the transferror court in W.D.TX and transferee court in D.DE.

 Semtek says that a subsequent state court must apply at least as much preclusive
effect as state in which the federal rendering court sat. Hart tells us Oklahoma state court
could apply more preclusive effect.

802386 802386

 If W.D.TX had proper venue/IPJ, Ferens suggests transfer is just changing
courthouse, and we could therefore apply Texas’ preclusion law. Texas considers all
independent bases possible to support a judgment as decided against the losing party.

There were two bases for Sylvania’s MFSJ in D.DE (Groucho’s weak password
protection and Groucho’s not using 2FA). The same issue is present in whether
Groucho’s failure to use 2FA makes him a causal factor in theft, the issue was actually
litigated and previously decided, and Texas’ application of general verdicts says it was
“necessary to the decision.” Texas would have IP run, thus Oklahoma must preclude.

 Alternatively, one could argue that Ferens’ holding on HCOL shouldn’t extend to
preclusion law because Delaware has greater interest in seeing their judgment respected
than Texas. Also, if W.D.TX didn’t have proper venue/IPJ, Ferens says apply HCOL
rules of transferee court (Delaware), and it follows we should apply transferee’s
preclusion law. In either case, apply Delaware preclusion law.

 Delaware uses FCL (Russell) and there were two possible grounds for Sylvania’s

MFSJ. Groucho changed his password and never wrote it down, which eliminates one
basis for the prior judgment. Since it’s unclear which basis Judge Marcal relied on
because she didn’t state her reasons in compliance with FRCP 56(a), Russell says IP
wouldn’t run in Delaware.

 Hart suggests Oklahoma could apply greater preclusive effect to the Delaware
judgment, and since Oklahoma doesn’t follow Russell, it might do so.
