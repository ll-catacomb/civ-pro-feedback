## Question 7

The hypothetical small claims court suffers from a number of flaws that suggest it is not
the best idea. The scheme suffers from constitutional problems and would create more,

rather than less, problems for low- and moderate-income people in accessing justice.

Despite some positive features, overall this small claims court would disadvantage pro se

litigants, create opportunities for bias and inequitable advantages, and stifle incentive for

structural change.

This small claims court proposal meets several constitutional requirements, but lacks

others. There’s a possible 7th amendment problem, since damages claims are traditionally

heard by juries. However, small claims courts sometimes don’t offer jury trials. The more

problematic issues concern procedural due process. On the one hand, this scheme

provides adequate notice. The Mullane line of cases require that reasonable efforts to give

notice, and cases such as Roller v. Holly and War Eagle Village held that a matter of days

is insufficient. Here, six weeks is given, allowing defendants plent of time to prepare.

However, we don’t know what the pleading standard of this court is. If it is more meager

than notice pleading, the complaint may not provide adequate notice.

There are also problems with how the hearing is conducted. True, several features

approved in Vitek and the Restatement of Judgments are present: notice, parties can enter

365008 365008

affadavits and evidence, they can receive testimony, and there must be a final judgment.

And while Vitek included a right to present and cross-examine witnesses, the Restatement

doesn’t, so it shouldn’t concern us.

But two important procedural features are troubling. First, the absence of any recording

of the proceedings, coupled with the fact that the rules of evidence don’t apply. If parties
don’t know the rules and have nothing to learn from, how can they adequately argue their

cases? The Restatement require a formulation of issues of fact and law in terms of their

application to the case; it should be required here too. Moreover, judges admiting only

what they see as reliable opens up opportunities for either bias or erroenous outcomes,

since it isn’t clear whether the judges will be competent to assess evidence. Which brings

us to the second problem, the judges: who are they? One of the fundamental requirements

of due process is a neutral decisionmaker. That doesn’t need to be a lawyer, but we

should find out who these judges are and ensure they don’t introduce unfair standards or

bias.

This scheme also creates problems for access to justice. First, by allowing lawyers into

the proceeding but not requiring them, the scheme disadvantages those who cannot afford

a lawyer, especially since the $5000 ceiling prevents attracting one. True, eliminating

motions, joinders, and counterclaims would help soften this asymmetry by reducing

complexity. And that most judges are not lawyers would mean they don’t speak the same

language as counsel. But lawyers are still trained in the law and adverserial process,

giving a leg up to their clients. They are also better at drafting affidavits and complaints

(if the pleading standard is Twiqbal, that will bar many unrepresented parties), examining

365008 365008

evidence, and taking testimony than the average pro se litigant. Moreover, since the

court’s rules are so fuzzy, repeat litigants will have the opportunity to learn what works

and what doesn’t. Finally, by capping the court at $5000 claims, not only will it prevent a

market for legal services from forming, but it will insulate wealthier people with higher

amounts in controversy from the negative aspects of this court system, leaving those

affected without political allies for reform.

For all these reasons, this system is not a good idea. A better system would eliminate the

involvement of lawyers altogether, and replace them with court-appointed advocates

trained in the court’s procedures. Clear, easy to understand rules regarding evidence and

procedure should be published and made available to litigants. Proceedings should be

recorded and made public, so as to provide a record to learn from and to make sure the

judges are behaving accountably.
