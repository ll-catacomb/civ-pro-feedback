## Question 9
 Courts should enforce a more robust right to relief from judgment per FRCP 60,
particularly 60(b)(4-5) (see Title). It’s incongruent to enforce (or claim to enforce) robust
procedural and due process protections during a trial, then to refuse to right a bad legal
determination when those protections fail to prevent the incorrect resolution of an issue. It
seems that unrepresented or poorly represented litigants are more likely to need this
relief, since they’re less likely to persuade the court on unclear/contested issues of law
that are later clarified. The countervailing concern is finality; courts worry about
relitigation with any change in the law. But the reason that relief from arbitrary/wrongful
enforcement of the law is crucial is because judgments and their consequences on
litigants’ lives are final. (Contrast Title with Hartford, where the Court held that relief
was warranted to preserve the integrity of public institutions, demonstrating the judicial
system’s eagerness to self-preserve while shrugging off consequences like wrongful

denaturalization as applied to people.) The expansion of summary judgment as a means
of dispute resolution will also protect against a flood of frivolous claims under robust
FRCP 60 enforcement. Effecting this shift would require a redevelopment of common
law on the issue, which is challenging but nothing we haven’t seen before.

 For an example of redevelopment of common law governing important trial
mechanics, see the summary judgment trajectory (with Celotex reflecting the Court’s shift
to encourage broadened use thereof). It might be worthwhile to roll that back. The pro-SJ

699952 699952

arguments are that it improves efficiency and helps avoid wasteful and unnecessary trials
- but is that true? We know from empirical evidence that the quality of cases that make it
to discovery post-Iqbal isn’t any higher than before, partly because judges are bad at

separating the wheat from the chaff; I don’t see why that’d be different here. Compared to
dubious efficiency/quality gains, the drawbacks are significant: Excessive reliance on
summary judgment threatens the right to a jury trial and allows fewer people their day in
court. It may be prudent to shift the development of summary judgment again through
common law, or to make other procedural changes to compensate, like allowing
immediate appeals under the Cohen exception. Redeveloping common law is a big lift,
though it could just take one case (see Celotex, or Iqbal throwing us back into code
pleading).
