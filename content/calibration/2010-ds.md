Answer-to-Question-_1b_

SIPJ would be a combination of power and discretion. 1) An 'anchoring claim' would be a claim in

which the defendant has MinContacts with the forum state such that the exercise of IPJ is consistent with

the DPC of the 14th Amendment (Int'l Shoe). (a) A plaintiff could join any additional supplemental

claims against that defendant, regardless of whether those additional claims would give rise to sufficient
MinContacts if independently brought. A plaintiff can also join any claims against any additional

defendants if any of those claims (i) share a CNOF with the anchoring claim AND (ii) the defendant

otherwise would have minimum contacts with the forum state (not necessarily with respect to the
particular action.

(b) There must be SMJ or SSMJ over any claims against plaintiffs joined under 1(a).

(c) The court can decline to exercise SIPJ even if proper if (i) non-IPJ claims predominate; (ii) IPJ claims

are dismissed before trial; (iii) another state has a compelling interest in adjudicating the SIPJ-joined

claims (iv) other exceptional compelling reasons.

I do not think SIPJ should be allowed. SMJ is meant to maintain Fed Courts as courts of limited

jurisdiction. SSMJ was meant to promote judicial efficiency. IPJ is meant specifically to protect

defendants from unfair litigation. Any weakening of this (through SIPJ) weakens the protections of

defendants and undermines the main point of IPJ. I will show why this is unavoidable.

Pre-1367, SSMJ was not statutory but judge-made, so it was based in the Constitutional power of the

judiciary. This power was limited case-by-case based on principles of judicial efficiency and general

principles (discretion). SIPJ would start with judges.

Considering the pre-1367 history of SSMJ, SIPJ has an uphill battle. Gibbs allowed supplemental claims

between the same parties, but the court was hesitant to allow expansions based on supplemental parties
(See Aldinger, Finley).

SSMJ promotes judicial efficiency and fairness by allowing similar cases to be heard in a single trials,

avoiding the expense of two trials both to the parties & the courts. SIPJ over supplemental parties would

similarly promote judicial efficiency by allowing two parties to sort out all their claims in one forum; it is

fair because that defendant will be forced to litigate in the forum state anyway. This might be approved

by courts.

But SSMJ and SIPJ are different.

The limit of 'MinContacts' on IPJ is constitutional; state long-arm statutes can decide whether to go that

far, but can go no further. IPJ applies to federal & state courts. Fed SMJ is a combination of

Constitution & Statute (ArtIII, 1331, 1332) and governs fed courts. Pre-1367 modifications of SMJ can

be seen as statutory interpretation within Constitutional limits. Changes to IPJ are substantially more

difficult and, as the long gap between Pennoyer and Int'l Shoe and the legal fictions created in the

intervening years show, the court is hesitant to enact changes.

1367 allows jurisdiction to the full extent of ArtIII (directly addressing Finley/Aldinger concerns with

supplemental parties) limited by 1332 (See Guaranteed Systems); if SIPJ were to confer IPJ over

supplemental parties without even MinContacts, then it would go beyond the limits of the 14th

Amendment as currently defined. The judicial interpretation of the DPC in the 14th Amendment would

be destroyed by an over-broad SIPJ. Even if SIPJ were limited so that it does not extend to any party

that does not have any MinContacts with the forum state it would still violate the tenant of IPJ that the

defendant must have specific MinContacts with the forum state arising out of the specific cause of action.

SIPJ would 'expand' the interpretation of the 14th Amendment (i.e., overrule most of Int'l Shoe & its

progeny).

Consider the current case. If there is IPJ over count 1, HP will claim SIPJ over Dean for count 2. We

would use count 1 as an anchoring claim and attach count 2, since there are no MinContacts from count 2

and Dean isn't subject to general jurisdiction in Maryland and couldn't reasonably foresee being haled into

court there for any action taken anywhere (Helicopteros). This may not be unfair to Dean in any

practical sense; he would have to litigate in Maryland anyway, and it will probably save him time and
lawyer fees to dispense with both lawsuits in a single forum. Unfortunately, though, this clearly goes

beyond the MinContacts test, since count 2 occurred in Massachusetts regarding a Massachusetts citizen.

Fairness & judicial efficiency can only go as far as the Constitution will allow, and we would have to

re-interpret the 14th Amendment DPC/MinContacts in order to let this work.

The court could use the discretionary power noted in 1(c) to avoid this. It's likely that Mass claims
predominate over Maryland claims and that Mass would have an interest in adjudicating this suit, since

HP was affected in Mass where he lives and is known. This would limit the power of SIPJ, but it would

not avoid the insurmountable problem of the conflict with the entire line of MinContacts cases

interpreting the limits of the DPC of the 14th Amendment.

Furthermore, it is unfair to allow SIPJ over Skeeter. She has no MinContacts with Maryland (see

Answer 1a). This would go against the entire point of MinContacts analysis, and would be more of a

"Six Degrees of Kevin Bacon" than a real supplemental test. The suit against Skeeter is not really related

to the first suit; it arises out of a different statement in a different state, and was printed in a newspaper

that was only read by people in Mass. That Skeeter set up the meeting based on an initial statement by

Dean in Maryland is far too attenuated to justify any exercise of SIPJ over Skeeter. SIPJ, if enacted,

would not bring in Skeeter; and enacting it would go against the point of IPJ in the first place.

Answer-to-Question-_2d_

i) PP's motion to stay is under Colorado River. Usually courts have an unflagging obligation to exercise

jurisdiction conferred on them, unless exceptional circumstances say they don't.
 Here, the Colorado factors are contradictory.

 Wise judicial administration/conservation of judicial resources points toward a stay.

 The state/Fed lawsuits are between the same parties on similar issues. However, there is a huge

difference in the damages requested and they might be different enough to militate against a stay.

 The suit here is products liability and the evidence is in Birmingham, so the court is close to

important evidence. The harm occurred in Mexico and the Alabama state court is not a more convenient

location.

 This is an issue of state law, but the Alabama court might have to apply Texas or even Mexico

law. It may be important to Alabama, since CRASH is an Alabama corporation, but the harm happened

Mexico and the plaintiff is Mexican, so Alabama lacks an overriding interest.

 There might be discrimination against the Mexican plaintiff since the defendant is an Alabama

corporation. But there are no particularly complex questions of state law (products liability is fairly
common) and the federal proceedings are not being used to restrain criminal or tax collection proceedings

in state court.

 Both cases are moving through discovery, and Alabama state court has failed to make any

significant rulings.

 In the end, a Colorado River stay is based on factors, not pre-requisites; and since this is an

exceptional remedy and the factors are not determinative, stay is not appropriate.

ii) CRASH's motion to dismiss on FNC grounds is a doozy. Which FNC do we apply; state or federal?

If state, which state?

 It is not clear whether FNC is substantive or procedural for vertical choice of law.

 I will assume under Texas law the suit would not be dismissed because litigation in Alabama is

not "oppressive", under Alabama law it would not be dismissed because that's the high court's FNC rule,

and under Fed Law it would be dismissed.

 Under York, this would be outcome determinative of this issue, since the Fed court would dismiss
and the state courts would not. Whether this is outcome determinative to the case is uncertain, but it is

highly likely, so state law would apply.

 Under the Hanna majority, first we note there is no FRCP on point. FNC doesn't conflict with

federal venue/transfer statutes (1404, 1406, 1601), because this is a motion to dismiss to a foreign

jurisdiction. We consult the twin aims of Erie. FNC differences would result in forum shopping, since

parties that want to litigate in foreign jurisdictions would go to Fed court, and those that do not would vie

for state court. This would lead to inequitable administration of the laws; so state law applies.

 Under Harlan concurrence, we consider whether this would affect primary conduct. It would

make foreign corporations less likely to sell products in the US if they couldn't dismiss on FNC, and
domestic corporations would likely not sell or open branches abroad for the same reason. Under Harlan,

state law should apply, since states are supposed to govern primary conduct.

 Finally, under Shady Grove, we have to see if the purposes of the Federal and State law can be

mixed. Even Ginsburg would likely have trouble mutilating Fed FNC rules enough to fit in with either

Texas or Alabama law. Alabama strictly prohibits FNC; Texas restricts it to the point where

convenience is no longer considered; just 'oppression.' The federal doctrine is so much looser it is

essentially a different rule. We should apply state law.

 All Erie analyses point to the use of state FNC law. Which state will not matter, since the case

would not be dismissed under Texas FNC law because it is not 'oppressive' for CRASH to litigate in

Alabama. Under Alabama law, FNC does not exist and the motion would be denied.

iii) A similar Erie analysis would occur for SoL, and the same result would ensue. Under York, SoL

are outcome determinative; under Hanna, forum shopping would result (see Ferens), under Harlan shorter

SoL periods would result in more lawsuits rushing to avoid time-barring, and under Shady Grove the

purpose could not be any clearer - 3 cannot fit into 2, and 2 excludes 3 (I assume SCOTUS cannot

mutilate arithmetic). We have no evidence that there is any federal SoL, so this argument might be moot.

 Horizontal choice is important here. SoLs are procedural for horizontal choice. Assuming
transfer from Texas was under 1404 (see 2b), Texas SoL will follow with the transfer (Ferens), and the

2yr SoL will have passed before the filing of the lawsuit and the claim should be dismissed on the merits.

 If the transfer was under 1406 (unlikely) because there was a problem in IPJ or venue in Texas,
then Alabama SoL will apply and the case can proceed. I don't see any reason that Mexico SoL would

apply for horizontal choice of law, since this is a diversity action and Fed Courts will apply the choice of

law rules of the states in which they sit and neither would apply Mexican SoL.

Answer-to-Question-_2e_

The dismissal couldn't have been under Alabama law because Alabama has no FNC and a 3 year SoL. It

must have been under either Texas or Federal law. As noted above, it is likely that state law applies for

both FNC and SoL. If Federal FNC were applied, there would be no preclusive effect since that isn't

dismissal on the merits. I will assume that Federal FNC was not the grounds for dismissal.

Issue preclusion won't work. We don't have the same issue. Alabama state court will apply its own

SoL, so that cannot be issue precluded by Texas SoL. If FNC is procedural for horizontal choice of law

purposes there can be no FNC preclusion since Alabama has no FNC.

However, if the Federal decision was based on Texas SoL law (very likely), then PP is claim precluded.

Claim preclusion requires the same claim (broad definition, TorO, [the same definition applies in Texas,

Alabama and Federal court]; here that is the exploded tire in Mexico, not the particular legal theory of
products liability or breach of warranty), the same parties (we have that), and judgment that is final on the

merits. Final judgment was entered, and under Texas law, a SoL dismissal is final on the merits. When

a federal court sits in diversity, the source of the preclusion law is federal common law but the content of
that law is the state preclusion rules of the state in which the diversity court sat (Semtek). A subsequent

state court must give at least as much preclusive effect as the rendering court; here a Federal court sitting

in Alabama applying Texas preclusion law would give PP's state-law suit claim-preclusive effect, and

subsequent courts would have to do the same.

Answer-to-Question-_2f_

This is not a prejudice class action (23(b)(1)), nor an injunctive action (23(b)(2)). This is a damages

class action (23(b)(3)). The requirements are numerocity, commonality (which becomes predominance

in 23(b)(3), a stricter form of commonality), superiority, typicality and representativeness.

We are in the "middle ground" of actions between the floor (25) and the safe number (40) for class

actions. We need to consider the geographic dispersion and size of the claims. Here, potential class

members are spread out and claims vary widely in size. This does not bode well for certification. We
need to know if the members are clustered anywhere, or if they tend to the larger or smaller damages.

We can find this out by contacting the parties we learned about from CRASH.

Typicality is met if the claims are breach of warranty, assuming each bike came with the 2-year warranty.

All of these claims should have arisen out of the tire blowout; the only issue is whether they blew-out

under different conditions. We can find out by contacting the class members. There are no apparent
issues with representativeness. PP's claim is larger than the other class members. The fact that he is

foreign might be an issue if he is more likely to settle to return quickly to Mexico, but we have no

evidence of that.

Since commonality is replaced by predominance, we consider whether common issues of law and fact

predominate among class members. This does not look promising; the blowouts were under a wide
variety of terrains and damages varied greatly. Individual facts may be important. We would have to

contact class members to find out more about their specific injuries.

Superiority is a problem since the claims are sufficiently large to encourage individual lawsuits, and the

geographic dispersion makes class management difficult. That 70% of the potential suits have already

been settled suggests a class action is not the best solution. As it stands, this should not be certified.
