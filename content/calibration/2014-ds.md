 But there may be an Erie-conflict between MARCP and FRCP, which doesn’t

 1(b):The commentary speaks to the state-interests involved here. The commentary

 1(c):Judge-made-common-law tells us what rules mean. The high court now tells us

 1(d):Stevens agrees with Ginsburg that we should look at state interests but

Though, granted, the distinction we are trying to make here may be like “"eligibility" v.

 1(e): I think that federalism and form-shopping rationales point to following state

procedure. I could imagine a system where federal courts had their own uniform

common-law, one which attempted to create cross-state substantive law, regulate

behavior in the way torts does etc. However, we don’t have that system (Erie) and believe
that states should experiment and regulate activities within their borders. Federal

diversity is only about giving redress to out-of-state-citizens, and nothing more. Given

this understanding of DJ, federal courts should avoid meddling with state interests, and

applying a lower pleading standard then the state will frustrate the state policy.

Furthermore, if indeed the state is right that requiring the report “pre-screen[s]” lawsuits,

(meaning that 26(a)(1)(F) actually does real work and may be dispositive in a suit), then
we will have forum shopping and inequitable administration of justice. Out of state

persons will go to federal courts in cases where they couldn’t go forward in state courts

(i.e. when they don’t have the witnesses) and this would mean that not all defendants

have the same law applied to them.

Can issue preclusion follow into a state with different laws? If not then no preclusion.

 Claim preclusion: We have the same parties, claim, and final judgment. However,

 1(f)(2):Preclusion law in general sounds very much like HCOL, but we say it is not.

 Question 3:Advocating: Twiqbal standards mean that “weeding” out occurs at the

complaint stage, where complaints are expected to make claims “plausible.” At each

weeding-out stages there are administrative costs, and once we have Twiqbal there may

be no reason to set up additional gates. Furthermore, the parties, under Twiqbal, already

expended resources in preparation for the complaint, and it makes sense to just let the

claims progress. Most importantly, if the complaint stage already decided what sort of

facts make the claim “plausible”(=could a jury find in their favor) then the discovery just

needs to bring evidence for those alleged facts, without re-litigating whether the facts
could support a judgment(NewRule56). And if jury finds in party’s favor, NewRule50

would just ask whether the initially alleged facts were proven. Hence, NewRule 50 and

56 just ask whether the boxes were checked-off/confirmed.

 Against: You could argue that Twiqbal merely shifted when we do what. Instead of

doing fact-specific-discovery after the complaint, we do much of it before. However, it is
hard to do pre-complaint-discovery without discovery tools. After the complaint new

facts will come to light, and it is because of those new facts/allegations that we need 50

and 56 to make updated determinations. Plus, 50/56 don’t require much judicial resources

—a judge just looks at the facts and decides—and so requiring them may be harmless.

 An intermediate position, one that I find compelling, would distinguish Rule 56

from 50. After Twiqbal, Rule 56 should be more permissive since discovery could still go

on, and the Twiqbal standard already established the alleged facts can, in whatever way,

support the claim. Per above, 56 should just be about checking off the boxes without

considering how all the pieces fit together. Rule 50, however, occurs after discovery/trial,

after new facts, theories, perhaps even amended complaints have come forth and cross-

examined in trial. At that point we need to “align” everything up. Is there evidence

supporting the complaint’s alleged facts/claims? An OldRule 50 would here be an

appropriate mechanism to see if the total record support a verdict.
