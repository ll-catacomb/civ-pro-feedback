Answer-to-Question-_1a_
The Fourth Circuit cannot hear this appeal until the merits are settled.

This is not a collateral order. It is not final as to the issue. (The judge can change her mind on this before

the trial is over.) The choice of law issue is probably not sufficiently important, since the judge believes it

is unlikely to come up again. It can be effectively appealed after the final decision. (Wasting time is okay.

See Lauro Lines.)

This is not an injunction.

Double discretion does not apply. The choice of law issue is a controlling issue of law, there is substantial
cause for disagreement (the judge just "think[s]" she's right), and an appeal would advance the ultimate

termination of the trial. However, the judge does not appear to have officially certified her order for

appeal. Courts tend to be sticklers for formalism on this so that the parties know exactly when to appeal.
Even if the trial judge certified, the Fourth Circuit would have discretion not to hear the appeal. It might

choose not to, since the judge has said the issue is unlikely to arise again.

An extraordinary writ would be inappropriate. If JJ is wrong, there is no evidence this is a common

mistake. There are other adequate means of review, no clear right to relief, and a chance to correct on

appeal. Cheney, In Re Cement. Also, extraordinary writs were generally about protecting appellate

jurisdiction (but see La Buy), not a concern here.

Answer-to-Question-_1b_

SS's best shot is to say that both the constitutionality of FGBRA and the choice of law question should be

issue precluded by JJ's decision.

For issue preclusion to attach, the issue must have received a final judgment, it must have been actually

litigated, and it must have been necessary to the judgment.

The issue was necessary to the judgment: JJ said the case turned on "business" not being interpreted under

Georgia law. The judgment is final, even if appeal is pending.

However, it may not have been actually litigated. If a plaintiff could win their claim based on either of
two possible decisions, neither is precluded. Russell, but see Kelley. In this case, JJ never said which law

governed. Since NF is from NC, JJ could have meant that NC law applies. (Assuming NC law would

consider NF's venture a business.) If SS's judge defines the issue settled by JJ as "is SS's venture a
business", then issue preclusion would help SS. If she sees the issue as "which law applies to the

contract?", he may decide that JJ never settled that issue.

SS is helped by the unlikeliness that JJ thought NC law governed NJ's case. The contract sued on was

signed in SC. Under the First Restatement, SC law governs. If SC uses the Second Restatement, SC has

closer ties to and interests in the issue, which involves the liquidation of a SC business's inventory

because of a bank with its primary place of business in SC. Neumaier (just persuasive in SC) also says a

plaintiff injured in another state by a resident of that state uses that state's law.

DB could also claim that JJ only ruled on NF's contract. If the contracts are treated as separate bonds, a

ruling on one may not apply to the other. Cromwell. However, in Cromwell the court found no preclusion

because Cromwell had never raised the "holder in due course" doctrine, not because he had never sued on

the bond in question. SS's judge could rule that JJ only settled the question of whether NF's business is a

business for the purpose of NF's contract. This would be a bizarrely narrow interpretation, since the

contracts were identical and NF and SS were equal partners in the business.

If SS can show the choice of law issue was actually litigated, this should be treated as an attempt at

offensive nonmutual collateral estoppel. Even though SS was the defendant in DB's DJA suit, the

underlying coercive suit was by SS against DB. One reason to reject ONCE in many circumstances is that

it encourages plaintiffs to wait on the sidelines and file once an issue has been decided. If plaintiffs can

use estoppel provided they wait for a DJA suit and countersue, they will still have an incentive to wait on

the sidelines.

In may ways, SS's use of ONCE is fine. DB likely fought the choice of law and constitutionality issues

hard because they could foresee future suits by SS or other borrowers. If SS and NF signed identical

contracts, DB likely used these contracts for other borrowers as well.

The procedures in the original federal suit were every bit as protective of DB as the procedures in state

court.

The preclusive judgment was not (as far as I know) inconsistent with prior judgments.

SS could probably have joined NF's suit.

To join NF's suit, he would have to claim that he and/or NF have an arising under claim. He could not say
that DB plans to mount a federal constitutional defense. Mottley. However, his breach of contract claim

includes a claim that his contract was amended by federal legislation. This could easily be included in his

well pleaded complaint. Even though his cause of action would still be state, this would let him use the

Smith exception. The federal issue is decisive; it is important. (The FGBRA is evidence that Congress

perceives a significant crisis. The constitutionality of that law may not compare in importance to the

constitutionality of efforts to raise taxes or issue bonds, but it is important.) And it is a pure legal (not

fact) issue. Empire.

If SS did not get into NF's suit because of arising under jurisdiction, he likely would not get in. Since he is

from SC and DB has its principal place of business there, he would break complete diversity.

Strawbridge. (His AIC was fine and would not be an issue anyway (Ortega).)

Since SC preclusion law follows federal law (Parklane), and since SS likely could have joined NF's suit

on an arising under claim, he will probably not be rewarded for sitting out by being allowed to use
ONCE.

## Question 2

This case is properly in federal court.

It has a state cause of action and doesn't fit the Smith exception, so it would need diversity jurisdiction.

The complaint requests more than the $75,000 minimum amount in controversy. At the time of the
complaint, a rational person could have believed this. NJ is from NJ, and JMB is domiciled in NY and

DE. Though the plaintiff planned to retire to NY, he had no definite time for doing so and his plans had

been destroyed before filing.

FMB was sued in its home state and should not have been allowed to remove. However, NJ did not move

to remand within 30 days. The procedural objection to SMJ was therefore lost.

If federal law governs NJ's certification request, he should get certification.

The case would be under R23(b)(3), so it will need to show superiority to other methods of adjudication
and a predominance of common issues of fact or law, in addition to the other class action requirements.

FMB seemingly has sufficient assets to meet all claims, so this is not a limited fund case. Multiple

lawsuits will not lead to multiple rules, since the different plaintiffs are not requesting different changes to

FMB policies. The case requests no injunction.

Numerosity is more than the 40 generally considered sufficient.

The commonality requirement should be seen as a "preponderance of common issues" requirement since

the certification request must be under 23(b)(3). The main problem here is that different plaintiffs would
be entitled to different awards based on which state they live in. However, differences in the state law that

would govern the claims if filed individually do not necessarily kill a class action if the choice of law is

constitutionally sufficient. Shutts

Typicality: NJ can receive remedies that would not be available to non-NY defendants if they filed

individually. However, NY plaintiffs form a significant chunk of the plaintiffs: 15% compared to 3% in

Shutts.

I have seen no evidence NJ would not fairly and adequately represent the class.

State law should probably govern.

Under York, the certification decision likely would not be considered decisive. In that case, the statute of

limitations decision immediately killed the case. A decision not to certify may be the death knell of the

case, but the court has rejected the death knell argument in another context. Coopers and Lybrand.

However, York has been largely superseded.

Rule 23 is a FRCP and likely valid. It may effectively expand a right (Rules Enabling Act), but to rule that

way you would have to say that the Rules Advisory Committee, SCOTUS and Congress (by acquiescing)

all erred. I don't recommend it.

R23 and NY law may not directly conflict. While Walker says not to construe federal rules narrowly to

avoid conflict, the Walker court did exactly that, and Gasperini made the narrowing policy explicit. You

could read R23 as allowing NY's limits on class actions. This resembles Gasperini--it protects the federal

interest in class actions while allowing a state modification not specifically precluded by a FRCP. It also

addresses concerns about influencing primary conduct and hurting Erie's twin aims.
The choice of certification rules likely shapes primary conduct. Companies will act differently if they

know they are not on the hook for class actions seeking statutory penalties. (Harlan's Hanna concurrence).

R23 allows inequity by letting NY class action filers in federal court get damages not available to filers in
state court. And it encourages plaintiffs to choose a different forum to get more damages. Hanna.

But which state?
To know which state, we have to know which choice of law rules to apply, what substantive state law

rules they would use, and whether they would consider the certification decision substantive or

procedural.

NY choice of law should govern if transfer was under 1404(a).

To know which state choice law rules govern, we need to know whether the transfer was under 1404. If
so, the transfer did not change the law. NY choice of law governs, since the NY federal courts must use

the choice of law of the state where they sit.

Transfer was under 1404(a).

Transfer is under 1404(a) if IPJ and venue were appropriate when the case was introduced. IPJ and venue
were appropriate, as JMB is domiciled in SDNY. The property in question is also in SDNY and a

substantial part of the events at issue occurred at JMB's Manhattan headquarters.

Under NY Choice of law, NY substantive law should apply.
NY has the closest contacts with the event. NJ was injured in NY by an NY resident. (Use NY law under

Neumaier.) Also, NY has an interest in avoiding foreclosures and arguably in policing its banks (see

Bergan concurrence in Neumaier). These outweigh NJ's interest in protecting their citizen's right to avoid

foreclosure in another state.

The certification issue is probably procedural under NY law.
I don't know NY law on this, but courts interpreting the Second Restatement (as opposed to the Erie

cases) seems to draw the line at whether the rule is contained in a statute creating rights. Class action rules
govern a general remedy scheme used to protect rights contained in other laws. NY might therefore call it

procedural.

NY certification law should probably still govern.

Since 1404(a) effects only a change in courtroom, NY law sticks. However, if "sticking with NY law"

means letting forum law deal with the procedural question of certification, you could use WV law and

approve a class certification. Federal law would allow one (see above) and WV law is more

certification-friendly. However, using NY certification law best advances the goal of avoiding forum

shopping by ensuring that no choice of law question hinges on a 1404 transfer.

Under NY law, don't grant class.

NJ has requested statutory damages. Under NY law, he can't get class certification. He would have to

amend his complaint to not ask for statutory damages, and then he would fall below the minimum AIC

and lose SMJ.

Grant a transfer.

Federal law governs this transfer (see Stewart). This case could originally have been brought in NY.
Hoffman. Borrowing FNC principles to see if a transfer is "in the interest of justice" (1404): NY is more

convenient for the parties, closer to sources of proof like the property and JMB's headquarters, and

consistent with choice of law. FNC also counsels deference to the plaintiff's choice of forum, and NJ is

the plaintiff. The desire to combine with WV's case suggests denying the transfer, but since the choice of

law in each case may be different and one settled, joining the cases may not be best.

WV's Case
WV's certification decision parallels NJ's (see above) with some differences that will make certification

difficult under federal law. The settlement leads to more concern about representativeness, less about
management. Amchem.

WV's class is below 40 plaintiffs that easily meets the numerosity threshhold, though above the 25 party

minimum. Finally, WV's class action could deny benefits to plaintiffs from states that allow a statutory

remedy. This could be seen as a loss of a constitutionally cognizable chose in action. Shutts. It may be

addressed by the settlement.

However, the WV law supporting certification would likely override these concerns.

State law governs. See NJ's Erie analysis. It makes no difference that WV's deviation from federal policy

is decisional, not statutory. Erie

West Virginia law should govern.

The final act (foreclosure) occurred in WV. Alabama Great Southern. First Restatement. Use First
Restatement, since a federal court sitting in diversity uses the choice of law rules from the state where it

sits).

Since the forum state is WV, it doesn't matter whether certification law is procedural or substantive. WV

law governs and allows certification.

Answer-to-Question-_3a__

I will assume that Jones' factual assertions about her own experiences are presumed valid unless

contradicted by the kind of documentary evidence available in Scott. Iqbal and Bell Atlantic did not

consider that issue. The weakest link in Jones' argument is causation, the issue in Bell Atlantic. This could

also be called a question about chain of authority, the issue in Iqbal.

As in Bell Atlantic, Jones clearly claims a link between defendant Clinton and her alleged adverse

employment consequences. However, Jones offers no direct evidence to substantiate the link. Her

circumstantial evidence is less likely than the evidence in Bell Atlantic to nudge the claim from
conceivable to plausible. The Bell Atlantic complaint describes behavior that would be difficult to explain

in the absence of a conspiracy. It also describes how the RBOCs could communicate in such a way as to

make a hidden agreement possible. Furthermore, Congress found the Bell Atlantic claim plausible enough

to investigate. Jones alleges no facts that would be particularly difficult to explain without Clinton's

action. She makes no claim about where or how Clinton got ivolved in AIDC employment decisions. And

where the Bell Atlantic complaint mentions a Congressional investigation, Jones refers only to a partisan

magazine article that is not exactly on point.

If the Bell Atlantic plaintiffs just pled themselves out of court, Jones may be safer. She alleges no

alternative (and legal) explanation of her facts comparable to the parallel conduct allegation made in the

Bell Atlantic complaint. Also, if Bell Atlantic only applies to expensive antitrust suits, Jones may be okay.

Enter Iqbal. In Iqbal, the plaintiffs pled no alternative explanation. They clearly claim that Ashcroft and

Mueller acted knowingly and with discriminatory intent. SCOTUS rejects this claim, not because it is
unclear, but because it is conclusory. Furthermore, Iqbal states that Bell Atlantic should not be confined to

expensive antitrust suits. Even if we ignore this statement and look only at the facts, we see a case with

very high political, foreign policy, and administrative costs. Jones faces the same problem. Her suit may
involve fewer important people on a topic less related to national security. But it involves the President as

a defendant. It is a high cost case, and the Iqbal/Bell Atlantic rule applies. A judge would grant a 12(b)(6).

Answer-to-Question-_3b__

I would allow Jones to go forward at the 12(b)(6) stage, but I would strictly limit and segment discovery.

Jones' claim would have been adequate under notice pleading (see Smierkowicz, Diogaurdi). Switching

from notice pleading to a looser standard (exemplified by Bell Atlantic and Iqbal) is a bad idea.

Discovery
Discovery can be a powerful tool for backing up legitimate claims that would otherwise be impossible to

prove. Jones would have no way of proving that Clinton called Dave Harrington to attack her without

discovery. She should get this chance.

In other cases where the substantive law demands proof of something not easily demonstrated, we find a
way to ensure that the plaintiff has a chance to prove her case. Title VII claims would be impossible

without forcing the defendant to talk, so we adopt a burden-shifting framework. Discovery ensures that

plaintiffs have a fair shot when they cannot make their case plausible without it. Jones should have had it.

If plaintiffs like Jones are weeded out at the 12(b)(6) level, we won't just shut down plaintiffs with

ridiculous claims. We will shut down anybody who has been the victim of an unusual offense that does

not attract scrutiny. Through Erie and other doctrines--not to mention the 14th Amendment--the law

attempts to ensure that similarly situated plaintiffs are treated equally (assuming, with Erie, that citizens

of different states are not legally similarly situated). The Iqbal rule privileges claims that have already
been the subject of factfinding by the press or Congress. Jones actually had a leg up because of the

American Spectator. If Congress had been on her side she would have had a better opportunity to prevail.

Discovery helps level the playing field to give plaintiff with less interesting or less politically relevant

claims a chance to collect evidence.

Discovery is a flexible tool. Through protective orders and segmented discovery, judges can reduce its
cost and its impact on parties and third parties. See Posner's suggestions in Marrese. The Easterbrook

article in Bell Atlantic suggests discovery cannot be controlled. If this is true, it should be weighed against

the rights compromised when 12(b)(6) motions are granted to avoid expensive discovery. Courts should

not consider a dismissal if the complaint meets the notice pleading standard and controlled discovery is at

all possible. Jones, for example, could be allowed to substantiate the link between Clinton and the AIDC.

She could start by deposingly only David Harrington. If that leaves a question to be settled, she can go

from there. This leaves the possibility that Jones would be thrown out because her first few deposition

targets lie, but the judge can evaluate this on a case by case basis. At the least, a judge should let Jones

interview a handful of people who might testify to a link between her job and Clinton.

Discovery can also be inexpensive. While a judge would need to set strict limits on Jones' discovery, the

process would be run by the parties. If discovery was originally limited to a request for admissions, a

small number of depositions, a limited request for documents and a handful of interrogatories, the court

could spend very little time on it.

Discovery can be used to harass a defendant into settlement or to harm third parties. Monica. Therefore a

court should be able to consider a plaintiff's motives when considering discovery (see Marrese). This
should be one of many factors considered when determining whether to grant a 12(b)(6). Jones complaint,

after it was amended to let her probe more deeply into Clinton's sexual history, might have alerted the

judge to potential harassment. However, on the hypothetical facts, Jones has not demonstrated that her
suit's goal is harassment.

The Dangers of Discretion
The Iqbal 12(b)(6) standard also provides a looser standard for judges. Under notice pleading, a

complaint could be heard providing it was not logically flawed (e.g. alleging something not against the

law) or absurd. Greiner on little green men. Judges tend to agree on the rules of logic and the lines of
sanity. By contrast, people disagree about the line between conceivable and plausible. Iqbal. The Bell

Atlantic complaint was plausible enough for Congress, not SCOTUS. Any standard that requires judges to

do more than judge logic and sanity leaves additional room for discretion. This is dangerous.

Courts have incentives to control their dockets. When they find a hole that allows them to avoid hearing a

case (Colorado River) they sometimes drive a truck through it (Clark). Clearer standards may not

eliminate discretion, but they allow appellate courts to intervene when that discretion is abused. See

Executive Software (it may not be law outside the 9th Circuit, but it is an example of how appellate courts

can use clear standards to stop trial courts from shirking). It would not have been possible under Gibbs'

less clear standard.
