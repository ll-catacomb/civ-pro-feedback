There is no inherent conflict with PDP’s focus on protecting the defendant and the idea that at some point proceedings get so complex or important that a lawyer is needed to achieve this. They are both factors to the same balancing test: how can the courts make sure to obtain the correct outcome with minimal harm to all parties involved. The introduction of appointed council is expensive –harm to the government – and complicates matters, thus decreasing the efficiency of the court system. However the introduction of a lawyer into proceedings is one, but not the only way, to balance the need for finding the correct outcome with the expense in doing so.

Having full jury trials could obtain the correct outcome for every matter in dispute. This is expensive so courts cut down on the PDP afforded in the name of efficiency.  Having a judge, not a clerk, make decisions (Sniadach) is more expensive but a judge will take more care in issuing decisions so have a lower error rate, justifying the cost. If the base error rate is thought to be very low, there is less PDP required. This also occurs when the pleading standards are high for the plaintiff and the proof needed can be evaluated objectively – medical testimony or documents instead of the credibility of live testimony.

When harm is minimal, such as with disability benefits (Eldrige), PDP requirements are relaxed but when irreparable harm will occur such as with need-based disability benefits (Goldberg) PDP must provide more protections because of the harm that would result in an incorrect decision. This is tempered against the harm to the plaintiff. In the interests of finding the correct outcome the defendant must have some sort of opportunity to rebut the accusations when there would be no harm to the plaintiff by slight delay (Fuentes). In contrast, when there would be harm by delay the plaintiff can proceed ex parte if a hearing is available soon afterward (Mitchell). The goal is to find a middle ground where the harm to the plaintiff is balanced against the harm that seizure would cause the defendant. If there is some way to mitigate the harm to one of the parties, such as posting a bond or posting a counter-bond, then the PDP will be adjusted to minimize the amount of work that party must do.

With all risks balanced how do you achieve the best outcome with the required level of PDP? Gideon remedy was providing council for criminal defendants, but overworked public defenders don’t substantially decrease risk of error; they simply triage and plea. Civil cases, which can be just as damaging as incarceration, should not follow Gideon in finding a feel-good but do-little solution of lawyers for all. They should give the litigant the chance to proceed with their case.

There are other ways to have mostly correct outcomes without expending resources on state-appointed council for all litigants, fitting into the balancing approach as a modified Eldrige test. Eldrige said the PDP, and thus the right to appointed council depends on the private interest, the risk of erroneous judgment, and the governments interest in preserving scarce legal resources. Courts usually find that the government interest outweighs the other factors when deciding categorically to not provide council in civil cases.

I argue that in addition to toning back the devotion to the government interest prong – litigants should have the genuine opportunity to petition the court or a civil legal aid bureau for appointed council for high impact but low monetary civil cases related to basic needs (Pollock) – a fourth branch of Eldrige is needed: the availability and satisfactory nature of waiving PDP to proceed with the case. If the litigant can waive some PDP to provide for a simplified procedure in exchange for a heightened risk of erroneous decisions the litigant should be allowed that choice. This would allow, for example, a pro se litigant to forgo motions for SJ in exchange for there being a speedy hearing on the merits of the case by a judge or administrator. There would be a higher risk of erroneous judgment, but this would be offset by ease of access.

This process must incentivize the government as a cost saving measure to set up a court system with PDP tradeoffs that is navigable by pro se litigants. The more pro se litigants view the process as fair, the more they would utilize it. The more the judiciary views the tradeoffs as unfair, the more they should appoint council for pro se litigants when they assert their rights to full PDP to increase incentives on the government for reform.

There will always be a bias towards litigants with lawyers. The goal of reform should not be fewer cases; the goal should be giving aggrieved parties the access the court system no matter their resources. Access to justice for all is key in providing trust in the judiciary: if a litigant thinks the deck is too stacked against them they will seek alternative redress.

The trial court had SMJ (diversity, non-AIC deficient). Under 28USC1291 the appeal must be after a final order. The R59 motion finished the trial so appellate jurisdiction is valid. There were two claims under R54(b) (Liberty Mutual), the negligence claim and the recklessness claim. There was no express determination that the negligence claim was appealable, even though it was separable from the recklessness claim. There is no Cohen exception under 28USC1291 on the issue of “mail delivery” because the issue was not collateral to the merits and it was reviewable later. Mandamus is not appropriate here because there is no flagrant abuse of discretion.

The directors should’ve appealed the “mail delivery” determination under 28USC1292(b) because Judge Jorkin’s writing stated that the controlling question of law of if Chudley had to have a permit was special, it is likely to reoccur with other drone operators, and the proceedings are very high-cost. There are substantial grounds for difference of opinion, and Judge Jorkin indicated this in the writing. Finding on the issue would materially advance resolution. However, the defendants didn’t file a motion within ten days so this approach is waived.

The appeals court shouldn’t allow appeal on the R59 motion for a new trial. The decision to grant a new trial is not reviewable except for the most exceptional circumstances, and the standard of review is a clear abuse of discretion (Yeatts). Judge Jorkin does not meet this high bar. The judge can make credibility determinations, unlike R50 motions, and this serves as a judicial brake to runaway juries. Judge Jorkin may have felt that like in Yeatts the clear weight of the evidence favored Fillius but not enough to succeed in JNOV, therefore R59 was the only way to proceed.

The JAMOL was properly renewed as a JNOV under R50 (Unitherm), but Judge Jorkin improperly rejected it. Under a R50 motion like a R56 motion all inferences are made in favor of the non-movant (Reeves). Unlike a R56 where the record is the evidence produced in discovery, R50 only looks at the evidence presented at trial. The appeals court would review this de novo. Doing so, all reasonable inferences are made in favor of the defendants. Thus, the emails directly contradicting the defendants are controlling. Therefore there is no rational way a jury could find that the defendants didn’t know about the accident rate of the drones prior to the FAA announcement and thus the R50 should’ve been granted.

The defendants could use claim preclusion against Slughorn’s offensive posture. She asserts exactly the same claims. This does not fall under the bond exception because there is no liquid market in judgment recovery. Slughorn is in privity with Filius; she substantially controlled Filius’s activities as the executive officer and sole shareholder (Gonzalez). The second trial produced a final judgment on the merits – Filius won and collected judgment. The state court must give as much preclusive effect as the federal court would have (Migra); the federal court had SMJ over the claims so there’s no Marrese problem. The policy behind claim preclusion is that judicial efficiency favors not letting parties have multiple tries at the same judgment; Slughorn can’t just keep filing suits until she gets all that she thinks she’s owed, spending judicial resources and the defendants’ time in the process. The interest in finality precludes this. Yes, the initial court may have been wrong, but it could also have also been wrong in Slughorn’s favor, but would she want to re-litigate then?

The defendant’s cannot use issue preclusion against Slughorn because she won, but if she is not barred by claim preclusion she can use it against them. The issue of timing of knowledge of Chudley’s accident rate was actually litigated and determined in the suit by Filius. It was necessary to the decision (Rios); it was the linchpin required to find recklessness and there were no rulings in the alternative (Russell). The issue was even between the same parties, see above, but mutuality is not required in NY.

Both claims are sufficient to put their respective defendants on notice (Dioguardi), but unlike the complaint against the directors the complaint against Chudley fails the plausibility pleading standard (Twombly, Iqbal). Absent the conclusion that Chudley was negligent in flying its drone through the forest all that is left is the allegation that there was a crash, Chudley was responsible. This is not enough to say that Tonks plausibly wins in court.

The court has SMJ over count I. The count is AIC deficient but there is a Smith-Grable exception. The question of whether Chudley needed an FAA permit is a contested federal issue embedded in the state cause of action that won’t open the floodgates into federal court. The answer to this question is a substantial federal interest due to the abundance of drone users and it is a clear issue of law, not fact. If this is held to be insufficient, claim I may be joined under R20(a)(1) because it’s the same T/O, even though it’s AIC deficient. Exxon green-lit this for two plaintiffs, one defendant, but not one plaintiff, two defendants. The policy for it would be that since it is the same T/O and one part meets AIC the case is obviously substantial enough to warrant a federal forum. The policy against it would be that it allows pulling minor matters into one omnibus case where different defendant’s interests could conflict to the advantage of the plaintiff who forced them all into one case together.

The court has SMJ over count II independent of count I. The parties are diverse – Tonks is a domiciliary of VA, Chudley is of DE (incorporation) and NY (nerve center). Both the compensatory and punitive damages put it over the AIC requirement. Adding the directors, NJ domiciliaries does not destroy this.

There is no problem with abstention because there are no concurrent state proceedings (Colorado River) or other issues (Pullman/Burford/Younger).

VA does not have IPJ over the directors. They are not domiciliaries of VA, so specific IPJ is needed. They didn’t purposefully avail themselves of the protections of VA law even though their company did. There were no minimum contacts with VA (International Shoe). This may not save them though since being hauled into court for your actions in a state where your business is ubiquitous may not offend the traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice (Volkswagen); their actions hurt all stockholders. VA though is not the best forum for litigating count I because all of the documents and witnesses are in NY (McGee), so count I will likely be dismissed unless the directors consent.

Count I also lacks venue: the directors don’t reside in VA (28USC1391(b)(1)), the substantial parts of the events didn’t occur in VA (28USC1391(b)(2)), and there is another district where action may be brought: NY (28USC1391(b)(3)).

VA has general IPJ over Chudley. Chudley’s presence in the state is so continuous and systematic that it is essentially at home in VA (Perkins).

Count II has venue under 28USC1391(b)(2) because a substantial part of the events occurred in VA.

Since the suit is filed in VA, HCOL uses the second restatement. Thus all procedural rules will be VA law but all substantive laws will be where the center of gravity of each issue is. The center of gravity of this case could be in VA (Babcock) since Tonks was flying the drone from VA. There is likely a better argument though that WV law should apply because the drone was part of the WV fleet, Tonks’s drone was flying in WV, and the crash and all evidence is in WV. Unlike Neumeyer, VA law doesn’t give Tonks more protections than WV law, but the idea that lex loci delictus should apply absent a good reason is compelling to prevent forum shopping. Therefore, the court will use WV substantive law.

Pleading and mutuality are likely procedural since they would not influence pre-litigation behavior. Pleading though may be ruled substantive; it is an important consideration as to how much knowledge you need when deciding to file a lawsuit. If it is, count II will also be dismissed for failing to meet WV code-pleading standards using R12(b).

The punitive damages will not be awarded under WV substantive law. Per Longbottom, there was no knowledge of the risk, and so no conscious decision to disregard it as required to award punitive damages. Chudley could thus file a R56 motion for summary judgment on that claim.

Count I will be dismissed for IPJ and venue, and also may be severable from count II. Count II, using WV substantive law, may be dismissed for failure to plead. If it is not, the punitive damages will be thrown out under WV recklessness law.
