## Question 3

(I assume here that issues of notice, jury rights, etc. described in Q1/2 would be
resolved.)

 As an access to justice matter, FLDRA offers two major benefits: simplicity and

efficiency. In terms of simplicity, the informality of FLDRA proceedings, with their lack

of complex rules of evidence, joinder, etc. make proceedings much easier for litigants to

participate in, especially those who may be unable to afford counsel. This is a major

benefit, considering that there is generally no right to appointed counsel for indigent civil

litigants, and that that right is unlikely to be granted in the future (Barton & Bibas).

 In Gideon, the Supreme Court wrote: “The right to be heard would be, in many

cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel. Even the

intelligent and educated layman has small and sometimes no skill in the science of law.”

While the court was referring to criminal matters, the quote applies equally to federal

civil litigation. But by reducing the complexity of proceedings, FLDRA’s structure may

help counter this ineffectiveness of pro se representation.

 The relatively quick and final decision-making process might also lower barriers.

Many litigants can’t afford to participate in legal proceedings that last months or years.

659473 659473

By simplifying cases such that they can be disposed of within a matter of weeks, litigants

who might otherwise give up their claims have an opportunity to seek justice. FLDRA’s

speed and finality also help address the issue of court docket overload.

 However, FLDRA’s design presents significant drawbacks. By eliminating most

evidenctiary rules and the practice of discovery, FLDRA gives judges greater discretion
to decide what evidence is persuasive, and strips plaintiffs of power to actually prove

their cases. Lassiter highlights that while the court cares little for litigants’ sense of

procedural fairness, it is concerned with reaching substantively correct outcomes. Thus,

we should at least be concerned by the possiblity that FLDRA, in empowering greater

judicial discretion in its procedures, also empowers greater racially/socio-economically-

biased outcomes of the kind seen in Lassiter. The finality of FLDRA decisions further

magnifies the potential harm of an unfair decision, as does the prohibition on party

joinder, which forces litigants to run the risk of inconsistent decisions.

 Is the scheme a good idea?

 I believe both that FLDRA is better than the overloaded, complex system we have

currently, and that it could be better still. FLDRA’s simplicity (no hearings, no

discovery) suggests that with modifications, it could be an accessible form of

adjudication for all. But greater procedural safeguards in the style of Turner should be

required. For example, FLDRA admits sworn affidavits as a form of evidence. Even as a

law student, I don’t know what’s required to turn someone’s testimony into a sworn

affidavit. If there’s a risk that the judge will dismiss improperly notarized testimony,

659473 659473

courts should have to send packets of information to each party upfront detailing the few

evidentiary requirements. Similarly, parties should receive guidance (as in Turner) on

how to determine what evidence their case will turn on. While these requirements might

increase the cost of proceedings relative to FLDRA’s current process, I think they strike

an important and appropriate balance in helping a greater number of litigants access the

justice system cheaply and effectively while reducing the risk of erroneous final
decisions.

Scalia-Hanna: Does §32 empower court to make SOL rules? Congress delegated

Caveat: is FLDRA FCL because it isn’t promulgated by SCOTUS? If so, look at “twin

aims of Erie” test. State courts can hear same cases as FLDRA can, so SOL differences

will lead to inequitable application of the law and possible forum-shopping as plaintiffs

seek more forgiving SOLs. Strike FLDRA’s SOL.

Ginsberg-Hanna: Same as Scalia here, because FLDRA/State SOL unavoidably
conflict. We have no basis for knowing what substantive policy a state’s SOL might

considered FCL rather than same level as FRCP. Under twin aims test, Ginsberg would

also strike FLDRA.

We can see especially in the Ginsberg/Scalia analyses that the outcomes depend on how

authoritative we believe FLDRA rules should be. Are they equivalent to FRCP, which is

owed greater deference than federal common law because its rules arise directly from

Congress’ delegation to SCOTUS through the REA? If so, the enforcement of FLDRA’s

SOL highlights judicial respect for the Necessary & Proper clause, which gives

Congress constitutional power to delegate to the courts.

OTOH, if FLDRA ranks below FRCP and is considered FCL, the FLDRA court should

strike down the FLDRA SOL, thereby respecting that a panel of 7 ALJs would be

overstepping their constitutional bounds in promulgating rules that substantively impact

state law and policy. Striking the SOL would evoke both federalism, by respecting states’

interests, and separation of powers, by establishing that FCL is owed less deference than

rules that the highest court in the land or that Congress itself promulgated.
