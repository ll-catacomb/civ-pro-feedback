## Question 1

Question 1(a)

Pince will be able to file for a motion to dismiss on grounds of jurisdiction and pleading

standard. For personal jurisdiction, MA federal court will exercise MA's long arm statute

under Rule 4(k)(1)(a), so we need to make sure we either fit the criteria if it's a specific

statute. The rest of this discussion assumes the statute extends to the limits of the

Constitution. It is unclear whether there will be general personal jurisdiction over Pince.

Her original domicile was in NH, but whether the domicile shifted to MA will depend on

her intent to stay. One argument that she intended to stay is that she went from moving

back and forth between NH and MA to settling down in one permanent shelter in MA for

two years. But on the other hand, most homeless people plan on being homeless

temporarily, and her condition lasted longer than expected, which is quite possible

considering the dearth of jobs during the economic recession. Regardless, there should
certainly be specific jurisdiction over Pince in MA because she has been studying a

program at a college in MA, and therefore purposefully availed herself of the benefits and

protections of the state. If driving in a state is sufficient for purposeful availment

according to Volkswagen, then living in a state should be as well.

For SMJ, for reasons stated above, it is unclear whether there would have been complete
diversity, and this hinges on whether Pince's domicile will be decided to be in MA or

NH. If Pince is domiciled in MA, there won't be complete diversity because Mungo is a

citizen of DE and MA (incorporation and principal place of business). Therefore, we

must look at the WPC (imaginary plaintiff) and see if there is an arising-under claim per

the Smith-Grable exception. Of course, if Pince imaginarily sued Mungo in the state

agency, the NMMRA would restrict him from removing to federal court anyway, but it is

unclear how this would be interpreted, especially considering that the D's right to remove

is a highly important right balancing the P's right to decide where to bring the suit. One of

the things the court may likely need to review in order to see whether Mungo owes

money to Pince is to review the constitutionality of the statute. At first glance, this case is

distinct from Smith because there the plaintiff questioned the constitutionality of the

federal statute, whereas here, it would seem that that issue would be raised by the

defendant's reply (not sufficient arising under jurisdiction per Mottley). However,

whether Pince has a negligence per se claim depends on the Model State Act, which is

rooted in sections 601-604 of the NMMRA. There is certainly a large federal interest,

given the scale of the program, both in terms of financial and personnel resources. The

"too hot" concern is weak because the ruling will either prevent future suits or return the

system to what it was before the enactment of NMMRA. Finally, this is a mixed question
of law and fact, considering that if the statute is found to be unconstitutional, the court

will have to decide on the issue of malpractice, according to the pre-existing state

substantive law. In summary, it is unclear whether the court will find arising under

jurisdiction.

Venue was properly laid according to 1391(b)(2), because the events occurred in MA, so
there's no need to rely on the questionable PJ, per 1391(b)(3). Next, Pince can claim that

the pleading standard was not met. The information regarding the complaint seems to

indicate that it did not adequately articulate facts and thus did not provide sufficient

information for the court to decide whether the suit would be plausible. As a result, under

the Twiqbal standard, the case may likely be dismissed. The motion to stay will not

succeed because there is no simultaneous state proceeding, which is a prerequisite under

Colorado River. It is unclear whether this would apply to a state agency proceeding

(addressed below), but there isn't one anyway.

(b)

This type of case should be adjudicated in federal court because of fairness and

separation of powers concerns. Considering that Ds cannot remove suits in state agencies,

this gives the P too much power (home field advantage, favorable agencies). Moreover,

exclusive adjudication in federal courts would preserve the right to a jury trial,

opportunity to be heard before seizure of property, and appeal (not available in agencies).

In addition, these statutes give too much power to agencies over a very important area of

law. Agencies are vulnerable to capture and the Decision Officer may not be impartial or
have legal expertise.

Leaving this adjudication to states will also serve the interests of fairness, judicial

economy, and federalism. Considering that federal courts require code pleading

(Twiqbal), many Ps will not be able to conduct the discovery necessary to meet that

standard. Finally, state law will be inconsistent and so state actors have more expertise,
and the state system is less clogged. Plus, agencies will be every efficient. On the other

hand, agencies do not allow joinder or class actions, both of which federal courts would

allow. Finally, this type of case is a mixed question of law and fact, rather than a question

of law, and so is best left to state courts for efficiency purposes (Grable). Malpractice

(tort) law is an area traditionally adjudicated by states and so they should have more

expertise (probate and divorce law is left to states for expertise reasons).

## Question 2

Question 2

It isn't clear whether Colorado River's prerequisite simultaneous state court proceeding is

met by a state agency proceeding, but allowing it to apply would make sense, given that it

would be in line with the rationales of Colorado River, which are centered around judicial

economy, expertise, and convenience of the parties. Here, though, there is a weak judicial

economy argument considering that courts are clogged and the alternative forum is an

agency, which has a much more efficient proceeding. In terms of deciding whether there

was malpractice, the agency likely has more expertise, although in this case, this

expertise is not too relevant, since it would be an easy case per the Model State Act.
Certainly, the federal court has much more expertise in deciding on questions of law,

such as the constitutionality of the NMMRA. Finally, the state agency proceeding would

be more convenient for the parties, since there will be no trial and it would be an easy

case for the agency. Moreover, like the McCarran amendment in Colorado River, the

purpose behind the NMMRA was to delegate adjudication of malpractice to state

agencies, so there is overall a strong argument to be made for the federal court to grant a
stay.

## Question 3

Question 3

We can contest whether there was proper PJ in the NH proceeding. It is unclear whether

there was according to Sec. 904(b), since we don't know whether the newspapers' primary

circulation was in NH. However, since the agency can exercise PJ to the extent of the

Constitution, Mungo likely purposefully availed itself of the NH market by advertising

(International Shoe). The fact that the agency ignores venue can be raised, but given that

venue is not constitutionally required, this is a weak argument.

The biggest issue that we can raise is whether our client had a right to a hearing. In other
cases, courts have found that when a defendant didn't get a hearing before or promptly

after the seizure of property, there was an issue of inadequate due process. In this case,

the defendant did not even have a "hearing," and the opportunity to contest is not

available until after seizure. Using the Mathews/Doehr test, there is certainly a

considerable risk of erroneous deprivation in the agency procedure, both in terms of

whether the agency will actually find a valid claim for malpractice and to the size of the
damage award. The plaintiff just simply has to state a dollar amount, which can be

unreasonably high, and does not have to substantiate it. In this case, the plaintiff

requested twelve million dollars, which seems to be a very high amount. The plaintiff's

interest is not very high, considering that he will be suing doctors or hospitals, who won't

likely become judgment-proof in the near future and have a strong incentive to pay

anyway if they lose because they want to preserve their reputation. Defendants certainly

have a strong interest in having a hearing considering that their facilities may be seized,

and as mentioned, the plaintiff's claim may be small and not warrant such a sizable

seizure. The government has an interest in efficiency and accurate adjudication, and

although requiring a hearing will result in less efficiency and more costs, it should still be

a much lower cost than if the courts had adjudicated, under the old system. In addition,

no judge signs off on the seize order, the Decision Officer may not have legal experience,

the proceeding does not have to be initiated by an attorney, and the D has the burden of

going forward, all factors that favor a right to a hearing.

The NH agency proceeding and the statute in general are also problematic because they

don't allow for removal and give an unfair advantage to the P, who can choose the forum.

In addition, given that the claim is for damages, a legal remedy, Mungo had a right to a
jury trial (remedy sought is main factor). The public rights exemption under Atlas

Roofing is not applicable here because the government is not acting as a regulator. Here

the government has created an adjudicative forum for suits which are entitled to a jury

trial, and according to Granfinanciera, hospitals, such as Mongo, should be entitled to a

jury trial.

## Question 4

Question 4

(a)

For the class action for an injunction, the requirements of numerosity, commonality,

typicality, and representativeness must be met. Given that Mungo is a large hospital,

worth at least 12 million, and the class includes past and future patients who will need

radiological work (injunction class is mandatory so all these patients are included), it

seems that there will be at least forty people and so the numerosity claim should be met.

All these class members' claims will hinge on whether the hospital complied with the
provisions of NMMRA, regarding the posting and review of the National Registry

records, under a negligence per se theory, so there will likely be sufficient commonality.

In injunction class actions, there is a presumption of cohesion (Cooper). All the class

members should have aligned interests, because by complying with NMMRA, the

hospital will provide a more accurate diagnosis. Some members may want damages, but

they can tack on these claims. However, the named plaintiff in this case, Pince, is a past
patient, who is currently suffering from cancer because of negligence per se. Many of the

class members are current and future patients who have not even incurred damage.

Therefore, Pince has less incentive to obtain an injunction because it won't benefit her

like it will future patients. Thus, the typicality and commonality requirements may not be

met.

(b)

In order to determine whether the counterclaim will be dismissed, the court will conduct

a vertical choice of law analysis. Statute of limitations issues are considered neither

substantive nor procedural in VCOL analysis and so a sub-rule regarding when the

limitations begins to tick will also likely be considered in-between (Walker). Under York,

the selection of the rule will certainly be outcome-determinative, so state law should be

used. Under the Hanna majority test, it will be difficult to narrowly construe the federal

common law and so state law should be used to further the twin aims of Erie. Under

Harlan concurrence, it is unclear whether the application of law will change the primary
conduct of the parties. Considering that patients don't find out about cancer for several

years, hospitals may have less incentive to comply with the NMMRA under a discovery

rule, so this would point to application of state law, but this is unclear. Finally, under the

Ginsberg dissent in Shady Grove, considering the state interest of protecting patients, as

expressed by the adoption of the Model State Act, it may be possible to construe the

PMLA to only apply in any action in which the malpractice is not considered "negligence
per se" under the Model State Act, but this is a stretch. Most likely, state law will apply,

but we can look to the Erie rationales for guidance.

Under the Erie rationales of federalism, separation of powers, and equal protection, it

makes the most sense to use the state law. Considering separation of powers, Congress

has spoken to the issue in creating the statutes and intends for agencies to adjudicate

these claims, so it makes sense to apply the rule (misconduct) that agencies would apply.

In regards to federalism, malpractice law is state territory, so this also points to usage of

state law. Finally, equal protection does not really apply here because a filing in state

agency cannot be removed anyway, so there's no advantage given to out-of-state

plaintiffs.

(c)(i)

It is unlikely that Pince can appeal on 1291 (no final judgment here), 1292(A)(1) (no

injunction here), 1292(b) (no controlling question of law issue), collateral order

(appealable at end of judgment), or writ of mandamus (no clear abuse). Rule 54(b) is the

best option.

Under Rule 54(b), Pince may have a valid case for appeal. Of course, she will need to file

a request for the judge to say the magic words "no just reason for delay". There are

multiple claims in this suit and the judge made an express finding on the counterclaim.

However, it is unclear whether the counterclaim is separate from the other claims.

Although it seems the counterclaim is tied to the original declaratory judgment claim, it
may be distinguished on the fact that the original claim is dealing with the

unconstitutionality of the statute, whereas this one is making the negligence claim. On the

other hand, if the judge is not adjudicating the constitutionality of the original declaratory

action claim, but rather whether there was negligence per se, then the counterclaim is

tightly wound in facts and law with the original claim and thus the appeal will be denied.

(ii)

This would be a situation of claim preclusion. First, the fact that the appeal is pending

does not prevent claim preclusion. But if the appeal is successfully granted in the first

suit, then the precluded claim can resume. The question will hinge on whether the state

agency will use the preclusive law of the state in which the federal court sits or the

federal common law. To have claim preclusion, four elements need to be satisfied: final

decision, on the merits, claim must be the same, and same parties. There was a final

decision, the counterclaim in federal court and the claim in the state agency are the same

"case or controversy" since they both will see whether there was negligence, due to the

hospital's failure to comply with the statute, and the parties are the same. Whether a

dismissal on limitations grounds is on the merits is ambiguous per Semtek, but here we

know what the state laws are.

If the federal court had reviewed this suit on diversity grounds, then Semtek tells us that

the agency will use the preclusion law of the state in which it was sitting (MA), and so

the claim should be precluded because the judgment was based on SOL. If the federal

court had reviewed the suit on arising under grounds, then it is unclear whether the

agency will use MA preclusive law (Semtek), or federal common law (Parklane Hosiery),
under which there would be no claim preclusion (not for SOL dismissal). An argument

that federal common law should be used is that it would result in uniformity. However,

this argument is weak, since a federal arising-under case would only arise in a Smith-

Grable exception, which is extremely rare. Thus, it would be more sensible to apply the

law of the state in which the federal court is sitting (MA), which would result in claim

preclusion.

(d)

As discussed above, the four elements of claim preclusion will be met for the agency

proceeding. According to Elliot, the federal court will use the preclusion law of the state

law in which the agency sits (NH). In order to decide whether the agency's ruling will

have preclusive effect, we must analyze whether the agency acted like a court and

provided due process (Restatement of Judgment, section 83). Using the mini-DPC

standard as detailed in Vitek, Mungo's was served notice, had the right to bring and

challenge evidence (section 512 of Model State Act), and the ruling was final. It is

unclear whether the D received a hearing in the traditional sense. He did get to submit

witness affidavits, but not in the context of a traditional hearing. Finally, it is unclear how

impartial the decisionmaker is. Thus, it is unclear whether the state agency was sufficient.
Thus, the counterclaim may or may not be claim precluded.
