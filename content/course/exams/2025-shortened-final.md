Georgia uses the Second Restatement for Conflicts of Laws. Florida uses the First Restatement.
All states relevant to this exam have long-arm statutes extending to constitutional limits.
Under Florida Law, a limitations-based dismissal is considered on the merits for claim preclusion
purposes. Florida allows issue preclusion without regard to mutuality, meaning that it allows both
offensive and defensive nonmutual collateral estoppel (so Florida has adopted the New York rule).
Under Georgia law and federal common law, a limitations-based dismissal is considered not on the
merits for claim preclusion. Georgia requires mutuality for issue preclusion but allows the parties to have
shifted procedural postures. So for issue preclusion to run under Georgia law, the same parties must be
involved in lawsuits one and two, but a defendant in lawsuit one can be the plaintiff in lawsuit two, and
vice versa.
Except as noted in the two previous paragraphs, all jurisdictions relevant to this exam use the standard
preclusion law we discussed in class.
On January 4, 2022, Congress passed the Limitations in Federal Courts Act (LFCA), which provided as
follows: “For any cause of action filed after the date of this statute’s passage in a federal district court
sitting in diversity, the applicable limitations period shall be three years from the last event needed for
the action’s accrual.”
Also on January 4, 2022, Congress created a federal tort cause of action that private plaintiffs can assert
called Unauthorized Computer System Access (UCSA). To prevail in an UCSA cause of action, a plaintiff
must prove the following:
• An employee of a state government or the federal government,
• accessed a password-protected computer account belonging to another person,
• without either the account owner’s permission or a court order authorizing such access.
An UCSA cause of action accrues when the defendant accesses the account, and that access is the last
event necessary for this tort to be complete. A defendant found liable for an UCSA tort must pay the
plaintiff $500 for each time an account is accessed. Further, Congress provided for exclusive subject
matter jurisdiction in the federal district courts for an UCSA cause of action, meaning state courts could
not adjudicate a cause of action under UCSA.[^1]
Georgia and Florida have a tort cause of action called Invasion of Privacy by a State Official (IPSO). To
prevail in an IPSO cause of action, a plaintiff must prove the following:
• A state employee,
• accessed a source of information that a person reasonably expects to remain private, such as a
diary kept in a secured location or a password-protected computer account,
• without either the owner’s permission or a court order authorizing such access.
An IPSO cause of action accrues when the defendant accesses the information, and that access is the last
event necessary for this tort to be complete. A defendant found liable for an IPSO tort must pay the
plaintiff $1000 for each instance of information access plus any actual damages the plaintiff can prove. In
both Georgia and Floria, the limitations period for an IPSO cause of action is four years from the last
event needed for the cause of action’s approval.

In early 2023, the Florida legislature passed a statute called the Limitations Demand Act (LDA). Under the
LDA, any person who reasonably believes that they may become the defendant in a civil lawsuit may
send the likely plaintiff a “demand to sue” letter. Here’s what a “demand to sue” letter means: If a
demand to sue letter references the LDA and demands that the plaintiff decide as soon as possible
whether they will in fact sue, then the plaintiff has six months to commence a lawsuit. If a lawsuit is filed
outside of the six-month period, the defendant may move to dismiss the complaint as time-barred, and
the dismissal is to be treated as though the statute of limitations had run. In passing the LDA, the Florida
legislature stated that the purpose of the LDA was “to reduce burdens on the court system from
dithering plaintiffs and to assure that lawsuits are adjudicated when the evidence is fresh.” No other
state has a law similar to the LDA, nor does the federal government.
Florida and Georgia have three federal districts each. In each state, the names are identical: the
Southern, the Middle, and the Northern.
South Carolina has only one district, the District of South Carolina. Massachusetts has only one federal
district, the District of Massachusetts. Nebraska has only one federal district, the District of Nebraska.

Suppose the Supreme Court grants certiorari on the issue of whether it should adopt two versions of the
Matthews v. Eldridge test, one for incorporeal parties (corporations, labor unions, partnerships,
unincorporated associations) and one for human beings. The idea is that the due process needs of
human beings might be different from those of incorporeal entities. Perhaps, for example, in certain
circumstances, given the Supreme Court’s holdings in Lassiter and Turner, for human beings the more
important the interest at issue, the less process there should be. The reason for less process would be to
render what process there is something that human beings could actually use. Perhaps for incorporeal
entities, who frequently have lawyers to represent them, the rule should be the opposite. So for
incorporeal entities, more important interests should lead to more process, the way the Matthews test
operates now. One possible implication of such a proposal is that it might also create a reform
movement to adopt a different set of non-constitutional procedural rules for federal district court
litigation involving only incorporeal entities versus litigation that involves at least one human party.
